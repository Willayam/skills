# W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne

Reference for the blue-ocean advisor skill. Facts and quotes were checked against the authors' site, INSEAD Knowledge, HBR, and press coverage. Unverified details were left out.

## Who they are

W. Chan Kim and Renée Mauborgne are professors of strategy at INSEAD in Fontainebleau, France, and co-directors of the INSEAD Blue Ocean Strategy Institute. Kim is Korean-born and holds the Boston Consulting Group Bruce D. Henderson Chair. Mauborgne is American and an INSEAD Distinguished Fellow. They have written together for over three decades, starting with Harvard Business Review articles in the 1990s that built the vocabulary they later packaged as blue ocean strategy. Their method is empirical. The first book rests on a study of more than 150 strategic moves across 30 industries and 100 years.

Blue Ocean Strategy came out in 2005, expanded in 2015, and has sold more than four million copies in 49 languages. Blue Ocean Shift followed in 2017 as the how-to companion. Beyond Disruption came out in 2023 and introduced nondisruptive creation. Thinkers50 ranked them the most influential management thinkers in the world in 2019, and in 2023 HBR named them two of the four leading thinkers in its first hundred years. Their argument has not changed. Competing head to head in an existing market is a low-margin way to grow. Creating new demand is the high-margin way, and it can be done by method rather than by luck.

## Core frameworks

### Red oceans, blue oceans, and value innovation

Red oceans are all the industries that exist today. Boundaries are defined, rules are known, and companies fight for share of existing demand. As the space crowds, products become commodities and margins fall. Blue oceans are market spaces that do not exist yet. Demand is created rather than fought over.

Value innovation is the engine, the simultaneous pursuit of differentiation and low cost. Conventional strategy says pick one, because more value costs more to deliver. Kim and Mauborgne say the trade-off only holds if you accept the industry's current competing factors. Drop the factors buyers do not value and cost falls. Add factors the industry has never offered and value jumps. Both happen at once. "Value innovation occurs only when companies align innovation with utility, price, and cost positions."

Their study of 108 new business launches found that 86 percent were line extensions inside red oceans. Those produced 62 percent of revenue but only 39 percent of profit. The 14 percent aimed at new market space produced 38 percent of revenue and 61 percent of profit.

Test any proposal with two questions. Does it lift buyer value in a way rivals do not? Does it drop the cost structure at the same time by eliminating factors the industry over-delivers? If only one is true, it is a red ocean move dressed up.

### The strategy canvas

A one-page picture of an industry's strategic profile. The horizontal axis lists the factors the industry competes on. The vertical axis shows how much each company offers on each. Plot a competitor and you get its value curve.

How to draw one:

1. List the five to twelve factors the industry competes on, in buyers' language. Price is usually one.
2. Rate each major player on each factor from low to high. Rough is fine. Shape matters, not precision.
3. Connect the points to form each player's value curve.
4. Look at what the picture says before arguing about it.

How to read a convergent profile. In a mature industry the curves lie on top of one another. Everyone offers a bit more of the same things at a slightly different price. That is a red ocean, and the picture usually shocks executives who believed they were differentiated. Kim and Mauborgne call this stage visual awakening. The other three steps in "Charting Your Company's Future" are visual exploration in the field, a visual strategy fair where teams present canvases to be judged, and visual communication of the chosen curve to the whole company.

What a good curve looks like. The same 2002 article sets three tests. Focus, meaning the curve is not high on everything. Divergence, meaning its shape visibly departs from the industry's. A compelling tagline, meaning one honest sentence a buyer would understand. Southwest Airlines is their standard example. High on friendly service, speed, and frequent point-to-point departures. Low or absent on meals, lounges, seating classes, and hub connectivity. The tagline in effect was the speed of a plane at the price of a car.

### The four actions framework and the ERRC grid

Four questions reconstruct buyer value and rebuild the value curve.

1. Which factors that the industry takes for granted should be eliminated?
2. Which factors should be reduced well below the industry's standard?
3. Which factors should be raised well above the industry's standard?
4. Which factors that the industry has never offered should be created?

The eliminate-reduce-raise-create grid records the answers in four boxes. Eliminate and reduce cut cost. Raise and create lift value. Most companies only raise and create, which drives cost up and is why so many "innovations" lose money. An empty eliminate box is a warning sign.

How to run it. Start from the drawn canvas. Take each factor and ask the four questions. Write specific answers. "Eliminate animal acts" is useful. "Eliminate waste" is not. Draw the new curve and apply the focus, divergence, and tagline tests.

### The six paths framework

Six ways to look across conventional boundaries. Each challenges an assumption red ocean strategy takes for granted.

1. Alternative industries. Buyers weigh alternatives that serve the same purpose in different forms. Yellow tail looked at beer and cocktails, not other wineries.
2. Strategic groups within an industry. Ask why buyers trade up or down between groups. NetJets sat between first-class commercial and corporate jet ownership. Curves sat between health clubs and home exercise.
3. The chain of buyers. Purchasers, users, and influencers differ. Novo Nordisk shifted from doctors to patients.
4. Complementary offerings. What happens before, during, and after your product is used? A cinema's blue ocean might be childcare and parking.
5. Functional or emotional appeal. Industries drift toward one. Swatch made a functional product emotional. QB House made emotional Japanese barbering functional.
6. Time. Take a trend that is decisive, irreversible, and has a clear trajectory, and ask what the market looks like when it plays out. iTunes was a bet on digital music becoming legal and normal.

How to run it. One path per small team. Each team goes into the field and returns with one or two canvases. Each path forces a different set of interviews, which is the point.

### The three tiers of noncustomers

Existing customers tell you how to improve what you sell. Noncustomers tell you where the industry's boundary is and why. Three tiers by distance from the market.

1. Soon-to-be noncustomers. They buy minimally out of necessity and would jump at a leap in value.
2. Refusing noncustomers. They considered the offering and chose not to use it. Something is unacceptable or unaffordable.
3. Unexplored noncustomers. They have never thought of the industry as an option.

How to run it. Size each tier roughly. Interview across all three. Then look for what the tiers share rather than what separates segments. Their term is desegmentation. The blue ocean is usually in the commonality.

### The buyer utility map

A grid of six buyer experience stages across six utility levers, 36 cells. The stages are purchase, delivery, use, supplements, maintenance, and disposal. The levers are customer productivity, simplicity, convenience, risk reduction, fun and image, and environmental friendliness.

How to run it. Mark the cells where the industry competes. The marks usually cluster in one or two, often use and purchase. Then mark where buyers face the biggest blocks to utility. Pain points in empty cells are the opening. More effort in cells the industry already occupies produces improvement, not a new market.

### The sequence of blue ocean strategy

A blue ocean idea must pass four tests in this order. Getting the order wrong is how companies build impressive products nobody buys.

1. Buyer utility. Is there a compelling reason for the mass of buyers to want this? If not, stop.
2. Price. Will the mass of target buyers pay? Use the price corridor of the mass. Look at the prices of alternatives that serve the same purpose in different forms, find the band where most buyers cluster, and set the strategic price inside it. Sit higher in the band only if the offering is hard to imitate.
3. Cost. Can the company hit target cost at that price and still profit? Price sets the cost target, not the reverse. If not, streamline, partner, or change the pricing model. Do not cut utility.
4. Adoption. What hurdles do employees, partners, and the public raise? Address them before launch.

### Blue Ocean Shift and the pioneer-migrator-settler map

Blue Ocean Shift is the 2017 book on process. Five steps.

1. Get started. Pick the scope and the team, using the pioneer-migrator-settler map.
2. Understand where you are now. Draw the as-is strategy canvas together so the team shares one picture.
3. Imagine where you could be. Use the buyer utility map and the three tiers of noncustomers to find hidden constraints.
4. Find how you get there. Apply the six paths, then the four actions and the ERRC grid, to produce concrete alternatives.
5. Make your move. Hold a blue ocean fair where teams present options and a broad audience votes. "The fair is designed to take the politics out of the decision-making process." Then run a rapid market test and launch.

Two conditions run under all five steps. Humanness, meaning the process is designed around people's fears and need for dignity. Fair process, meaning engagement, explanation, and clear expectations, so people execute a strategy they did not personally choose.

The pioneer-migrator-settler map plots a portfolio by value curve, not by revenue or growth. Settlers are me-too offerings whose curves match the industry. Migrators are better than most but inside the industry's shape. Pioneers offer unprecedented value on divergent curves. Draw the map for today and for three years out. A portfolio of settlers and migrators is a growth problem hiding behind healthy revenue. Pick the blue ocean initiative from the businesses with the most to gain by moving toward pioneer.

### Nondisruptive creation

Beyond Disruption, 2023, adds a third kind of market creation. Disruptive creation solves an existing problem better or cheaper inside an existing industry and displaces incumbents. Blue ocean strategy creates a new market across industry boundaries and displaces only partially. Nondisruptive creation is "the creation of a brand-new market outside or beyond the boundaries of existing industries." Nobody is displaced because nobody was serving that need. Viagra replaced no drug. Square Reader let small merchants accept cards for the first time, and Visa and Mastercard gained business. Kickstarter, Sesame Street, microfinance, e-sports, and life coaching are their other standard examples.

How it differs from Christensen. Christensen's disruption starts at the low end or with a new-market foothold and moves upmarket to displace incumbents. It is a win-lose theory about existing industries. Kim and Mauborgne say that is one path, not the only one, and that "you don't have to disrupt to innovate." Mauborgne's line in Fortune was "Theories help us see, but they also act as blinders." They are adding a category, not replacing his theory.

Why it matters for a large company. Nondisruptive moves do not cannibalize existing revenue, so internal resistance drops. Pfizer's people were energized by Viagra rather than fearful, because it built a new engine without touching existing drugs. Backlash from regulators and partners also drops because no one is being put out of business. For an incumbent with a strong brand and distribution, this is the growth path the organization will support.

How to find them. Two starting points from the book. Existing problems no industry has addressed, such as Grameen's microfinance for people banks ignored. Newly emerging problems outside every industry's boundary. In both cases, "Nondisruptive creators think value innovation first, and then technology."

### Red ocean traps

The 2015 HBR article names six mental models that keep big companies competing on the same axes even when they say they want to create markets.

1. Seeing market creation as customer-oriented. Existing customers produce better versions of what you have. New demand comes from noncustomers.
2. Treating market creation as niche strategy. Finer segments produce small markets. Market creation desegments by finding what buyer groups share.
3. Confusing technology innovation with market creation. "Market creation is not inevitably about technological innovation." The link between technology and buyer value is what matters.
4. Equating creative destruction with market creation. Much new demand displaces nothing.
5. Equating market creation with differentiation. Differentiation alone raises cost and price.
6. Equating market creation with low cost. Low cost alone leaves value on the table.

Use the list as a diagnostic on any growth plan. Most plans in optimized companies sit in traps one and three.

## Teaching stories

Cirque du Soleil. Founded in 1984 by Quebec street performers while the circus industry was in decline. It eliminated star performers, animal acts, aisle concessions, and multiple arenas. It reduced slapstick and danger. It created themes, refined staging, original music, and multiple productions. That cut cost and drew adults and corporate clients paying several times a circus ticket. Theatre plus circus, path one. In under twenty years it reached revenue Ringling took a century to hit.

Yellow tail. Casella Wines launched it in the United States in 2001. It eliminated wine jargon, aging claims, and above-the-line marketing. It reduced the range to one red and one white. It raised retailer involvement and created easy drinking, easy selection, and fun. Its noncustomers were beer and cocktail drinkers. Within two years it was the fastest-growing brand in American and Australian wine history and the top import in the United States.

Southwest Airlines. The standard canvas example. Only friendly service, speed, and frequent point-to-point departures, with meals, lounges, seat classes, and hub connectivity dropped. That broke the trade-off between the speed of flying and the price and flexibility of driving. People who would have driven flew.

Nintendo Wii. Nintendo was last in the console war and analysts told it to exit. Sony and Microsoft ran focus groups with gamers. Nintendo studied non-gamers. The Wii eliminated high-definition graphics, fast chips, and complex controllers and created motion control and approachable games. It sold at a profit per unit from launch and at its peak outsold PlayStation and Xbox combined.

Nintendo Switch. Smartphones took the Wii's casual buyers, and the Wii U copied the tablet badly and failed. The Switch, launched in 2017, looked across boundaries again. A console that is also a handheld, played alone or shared in the room. It became the only Nintendo console to outsell the Wii. Blue oceans get imitated and the process has to be rerun.

NetJets. Fractional jet ownership from 1986, path two, between first-class commercial travel and owning a corporate jet. Buyers get a sixteenth of a plane, the convenience of private flight, and a cost close to commercial business travel. A multibillion-dollar market that did not exist.

Curves. Women's fitness franchise that grew fast from 1995, sitting between health clubs and home exercise programs. Thirty-minute circuits, no mirrors, no men, no complex machines, a small footprint, and a low monthly price. It reached women who found gyms intimidating and home exercise lonely.

Novo Nordisk. In the 1980s insulin makers competed on purity and sold to doctors. Novo Nordisk looked down the buyer chain to patients, path three, and launched NovoPen in 1985, a pen injector that removed the syringe and the embarrassment. It repositioned itself as a diabetes care company and led the insulin pen market.

Comic Relief. Red Nose Day started in the UK in 1988 in a charity sector with rising costs and donor fatigue. It eliminated year-round fundraising, galas, mailings, and charity shops. It reduced guilt-based appeals. It raised fun, recognition for small gifts, and transparency. It created a biannual televised event and a one-pound red nose. Tagline, "Doing something funny for money." It reached children and people on modest incomes, hit 96 percent brand awareness, and has raised over a billion pounds with no credible imitator.

Prodigy Finance. Started in 2007 by INSEAD students who could not get loans to study abroad. It lends to international graduate students at top schools with no co-signer, underwriting on future earnings. No bank served that group, so nothing was displaced. Their clean case of nondisruptive creation.

Viagra. Pfizer's blood pressure compound turned out to treat erectile dysfunction, a problem no drug company had addressed. Launched in 1998, it created a lifestyle drug market worth tens of billions without cannibalizing any Pfizer product, which is why the organization got behind it.

Square. The 2009 card reader that plugged into a phone. Small merchants and individuals who could never accept cards now could. Card networks gained volume they never had. Square used the iPhone rather than inventing hardware. Nondisruptive creation on someone else's platform.
## Their voice and characteristic phrases

They write in plain declarative sentences and repeat their oppositions until they stick. Compete versus create. Red versus blue. Either-or versus both-and. Customers versus noncustomers. The tone is that of two researchers who have looked at a century of data and are mildly surprised the rest of us have not.

1. "The only way to beat the competition is to stop trying to beat the competition." Blue Ocean Strategy, 2005.
2. "Competing in overcrowded industries is no way to sustain high performance. The real opportunity is to create blue oceans of uncontested market space." HBR, 2004.
3. "Create uncontested market space and make the competition irrelevant." Subtitle and refrain of the first book.
4. "Value innovation occurs only when companies align innovation with utility, price, and cost positions." Blue Ocean Strategy, 2005.
5. "Noncustomers, not customers, hold the greatest insight into an industry's pain points and points of intimidation that limit the size and boundary of the industry." Blue Ocean Shift material.
6. "A focus on the competition too often anchors companies in the red ocean, and puts the competitors, rather than the customer, at the core of strategy." Blue Ocean Strategy site.
7. "Blue ocean strategy is not about finding a better or lower-cost solution to the existing problem of an industry, both of which trigger disruption and displacement of existing products and services." Blue Ocean Strategy, expanded edition, 2015.
8. "Market creation is not inevitably about technological innovation." Red Ocean Traps, HBR, 2015.
9. "The fair is designed to take the politics out of the decision-making process." Blue Ocean Shift, 2017.
10. "There are no losers, and no market player is made worse off." Beyond Disruption, 2023.
11. "Nondisruptive creators think value innovation first, and then technology." Beyond Disruption, 2023.
12. "Don't ask what the business model and technology should be; ask what they could be." Beyond Disruption, 2023.
13. "Not all people are equally creative. But most are creative enough." Beyond Disruption, 2023.
14. "You don't have to disrupt to innovate. There is an alternative path that is often overlooked, where business and society can thrive together." Kim and Mauborgne in Fortune, 2023.
15. "Theories help us see, but they also act as blinders." Mauborgne in Fortune, 2023.

## Questions they would ask

For someone bringing a growth problem in a large, already-optimized consumer platform with a strong brand.

1. Draw your strategy canvas against your two closest rivals. If we covered the logos, could we tell the curves apart?
2. Which factors you compete on exist because buyers value them, and which because a competitor added them and you matched?
3. What is in your eliminate box? If it is empty, you have a roadmap, not a strategy.
4. On the pioneer-migrator-settler map, what share of revenue comes from settlers, and what share of investment goes to them?
5. Who are your tier two noncustomers, the people who tried you and left or looked and refused? Who has talked to them this year?
6. Who are your tier three noncustomers, the people who get the job done through something that is not a platform at all? What do the tiers share?
7. Which of the 36 cells on the buyer utility map do you occupy? Which cells hold the biggest frustrations, and are any of them empty for the whole industry?
8. What alternatives from other industries does your buyer weigh, and where do their prices sit?
9. Which of the six red ocean traps is your growth plan in? Most plans in your situation are in trap one or three.
10. Which problem in your buyers' lives does nobody serve, adjacent to what you do, that your brand gives you permission to solve? That is a nondisruptive move, and your organization will not fight it.
11. If you created a new market, what existing revenue does it touch? If none, why has nobody proposed it? If some, who inside will kill it?
12. Have you run a process, or only had a conversation? Who has been in the field with noncustomers, and when is the fair?

## When software costs fall 10x to 100x

Kim and Mauborgne have not written about agentic coding. What follows applies their frameworks to it. They hold up because they were never about technology.

The value-cost trade-off. Their claim is that the trade-off breaks when you reconstruct which factors you compete on. Cheap software attacks it from the other side. The cost of raising and creating factors collapses. Two consequences follow. First, every rival can raise and create just as cheaply, so those two actions stop producing divergence and the industry's curves converge faster. Second, eliminate and reduce become the only durable moves, because they are choices about what not to do, and cheap build capacity does not make that choice for you. Advantage shifts from the ability to build to the discipline to leave things out. The focus test gets harder to pass and worth more when passed.

The strategy canvas. Expect the number of factors on any canvas to grow, because adding one is nearly free. A canvas with 30 factors is unreadable, and the company that redraws it with six and a tagline wins the buyer's attention. Alternative curves can now be tested in the market, and the strategy fair can be run with working prototypes.

The three tiers of noncustomers. This is where cheap software does most of its work. Tier two refused because of price, complexity, or a missing piece, and all three are now cheap to fix. Tier three was unexplored because serving them looked like a separate business at full cost. It no longer does. The new risk is the reverse of the old one. It is now too cheap to say no, so a company ships a hundred niche products and falls into trap two. Find what the tiers share, then build one thing.

The six paths. Path four, complementary offerings, gains the most. The before, during, and after chain around a core product can be filled in by the platform owner rather than left to partners. Path six, time, deserves the hardest thinking. The decisive, irreversible trend is that software becomes abundant. Ask what buyers value when the software itself is free. The honest answer is trust, distribution, data, brand, and taste. A large consumer platform has all five. Its rivals will have the software.

Nondisruptive creation. Large companies avoid markets outside their boundaries because the bet is expensive and the organization resists. When building costs a tenth as much, the bet is small enough to run several at once, and nondisruptive moves do not start a cannibalization fight. A brand with permission to solve an adjacent unserved problem can now afford to. Think value first and technology second. Cheap technology makes that advice more urgent, because the temptation to build first has never been stronger.

The sequence. Buyer utility still comes first and nothing about cheap software changes it. The cost step is easier to pass, so the strategic price can sit lower in the corridor and the mass of buyers grows. Adoption hurdles change shape. Employee resistance drops for nondisruptive moves. Partner resistance rises if the platform builds what partners used to supply.
