# Hamilton Helmer

## Who he is

Hamilton Helmer is an economist who turned strategy into something you can test against cash flows. He took a BA in economics at Williams College and a PhD in economics at Yale, then joined Bain & Company. He left to found Helmer & Associates, later renamed Deep Strategy, and led more than 200 strategy projects for clients including Adobe, Coursera, Hewlett-Packard, Netflix, and Spotify. He taught business strategy in Stanford's economics department for ten years, ending in 2018. He co-founded Strategy Capital, where he is co-chief investment officer and runs a public equity portfolio using the same framework he sells to CEOs. His partner there, Chenyi Shi, co-authors his newer work on platforms and on building a second business.

He published 7 Powers in 2016. Reed Hastings wrote the foreword and describes a 2004 courtesy visit from Helmer, then a hedge fund investor, who skipped the usual questions and "started with a crisp overview of Power Dynamics." Hastings later brought him in to teach Netflix's leadership. Daniel Ek's blurb reads "At Spotify the 7 Powers are widely used as we discuss new initiatives." Helmer's stance is that strategy is a branch of economics, that a useful theory must be simple and exhaustive, and that if you cannot name which power you have and what stops a well-run competitor from taking it, you have a hope, not a strategy.

## Core frameworks

### Power defined

Helmer's definitions are short and he expects you to use them exactly. Strategy is "the study of the fundamental determinants of potential business value." Power is "the set of conditions creating the potential for persistent differential returns." His one-line mantra for a strategy is "a route to continuing Power in significant markets."

Power has two parts and needs both. The benefit must "materially augment cash flow," through higher prices, lower costs, or lower investment needs. The barrier must make that benefit persist by stopping competitors, existing and potential, direct and functional, from "engaging in value-destroying arbitrage." A benefit without a barrier is operational excellence, and any well-resourced competitor matches it. A barrier without a benefit is a locked door on an empty room. He says the benefit is common and the barrier is the rare thing.

The fundamental equation of strategy is Value = Market Size × Power. It gives Helmer two questions for any business. How big can this market get? What prevents the arbitrage? A large market with no power gives you a commodity. Strong power in a tiny market gives you a hobby.

How to run it. Take each advantage the business claims. Write the benefit in cash-flow terms, with a number if you can. Name the barrier mechanism, not a feeling. Then ask what a well-funded, well-managed competitor would do over three years. If the honest answer is "copy it," there is no power. If it is "they could copy it but it would wreck their existing business," you have counter-positioning. If it is "they cannot get it at any price," you have a cornered resource.

### The seven powers

Scale economies. "Declining unit costs with increased business size." The benefit is lower cost per unit. The barrier is that a follower would have to buy share at prices that destroy value, and would expect retaliation, so the share-gain math never works. The canonical case is Netflix originals. "Originals rendered content a fixed cost, guaranteeing powerful Scale Economies, and permanently altered Netflix's bargaining position with content owners." Test whether the cost is fixed relative to volume and whether a follower has any plausible way to reach comparable volume.

Network economies. "A business in which the value realized by a customer increases as the installed base increases." The benefit is that the leader can charge more or win more customers for the same product. The barrier is the cost of gaining share against a larger installed base, prohibitive past the tipping point. "If another firm gets to the tipping point before you, then the game is over." The power is "bounded by the character of the network." With Chenyi Shi he added two tests. Multihoming arbitrages away the differential value, and heterogeneity of preferences is the best predictor of whether scale ever turns into power.

Counter-positioning. "A newcomer adopts a new, superior business model which the incumbent does not mimic due to anticipated damage to their existing business." The benefit is the economics of the better model. The barrier is collateral damage to the incumbent's base business. Vanguard against Fidelity is the book's case. Covered in depth below.

Switching costs. "The value loss expected by a customer from switching to alternate suppliers for additional purchases." The benefit is the ability to charge more for follow-on purchases. The barrier is that a competitor must compensate the customer for the loss, which usually costs more than the business is worth. Three kinds, financial, procedural, and relational. SAP is his example. Switching costs only accrue on customers you already have, so they are built during takeoff and defended after.

Branding. "The durable attribution of higher value to an objectively identical offering that arises from historical information about the seller." The benefit is a price premium or lower price sensitivity. The barrier is hysteresis. "A strong brand can only be created over a lengthy period of reinforcing actions," and that time is the barrier. Tiffany sells an engagement ring for about double what the same diamond costs at Costco. "The biggest pitfall lies in diminishing the brand by releasing products which deviate from, or damage, the brand image."

Cornered resource. "Preferential access at attractive terms to a coveted asset that can independently enhance value." The benefit is whatever the asset produces. The barrier is law, such as a patent, or personal choice. Five tests: idiosyncratic, non-arbitraged, transferable, ongoing, sufficient. Pixar's Brain Trust is the example. The people could have left for more money. "The Barrier was personal choice."

Process power. "Operational excellence, plus hysteresis." Formally, "embedded company organization and activity sets which enable lower costs and/or superior product, and which can be matched only by an extended commitment." The benefit is cost or quality. The barrier is complexity and opacity that take years to match. Toyota is the example. Helmer stresses how rare this one is. Most claims of process power are operational excellence with a better name.

### The power progression

Helmer dates every power to a stage of the market, measured by revenue growth. Origination is before the market takes off. Takeoff is explosive growth, roughly above 30 to 40 percent a year. Stability is what comes after growth slows.

Counter-positioning and cornered resource are established at origination. "Counter-Positioning and Cornered Resource are most likely established in the origination stage. These are durable types of Power specifically because your route to Power is locked in early, so long as you execute well."

Scale economies, network economies, and switching costs are established during takeoff. Takeoff is "the stage when differential customer acquisition can take place at favorable terms; when there is high flux that makes normal arbitrage lags material to outcome." Once the market settles, buying share becomes value-destroying and these opportunities "disappear forever afterward if unrealized."

Process power and branding are established in stability. "Only when a company has scaled sufficiently and operated long enough can it have evolved processes sufficiently complex or opaque to defy speedy emulation." Brands need the same time.

Timing decides which power a company can build because each barrier depends on a condition that only exists at one stage. You cannot gain share at favorable terms in a stable market. You cannot have decades of reinforcing actions in year two. How to run it. Date the stage of your market honestly. List only the powers that stage allows. If you are in stability with no power, your options are the slow powers or a new market where you can be at origination again.

### Statics vs dynamics

Helmer splits strategy into two questions. Statics, "being there," asks what makes Intel's microprocessor business durably valuable. Dynamics, "getting there," asks what developments produced that state.

Dynamics start with invention. "Power arrives only on the heels of invention. If you want your business to create value, then action and creativity must come foremost." The invention can be a product, a process, a business model, or a brand. To create a significant market it must be drastically better than alternatives, which he calls compelling value. Three paths lead there. Capabilities-led, where you have a technology and hunt for the need, with Adobe Acrobat as the case. Customer-led, where the need is known and the technology is uncertain, with Corning's optical fiber. Competitor-led, where you must out-execute an existing product, with Sony's PlayStation.

Compelling value alone is not enough. "Most inventions don't assure Power." The sequence is invention, then compelling value, then a route to power. He and Shi later sharpened this. "If you asked me a couple of years ago, I would have said you get product market fit and then you figure out strategy. Well, that's wrong, actually." The choice of product shapes which powers are reachable, so the power question belongs inside the product decision. "The product market fit and power questions are different questions."

On operational excellence he is blunt. "Operational excellence is essential and constantly challenging; it rightfully occupies the lion's share of management's time. Unfortunately, it does not by itself assure differential margins." Planning is no substitute either. "Planning rarely creates Power. It may meaningfully boost Power once you have established it, but if Power does not yet exist, you can't rely on planning."

### Counter-positioning in depth

This is the one power an incumbent cannot easily answer. Helmer coined the term because existing frameworks blamed incumbents for stupidity, and his point is the opposite. The incumbent's failure "results from thoughtful calculation." When the incumbent runs the numbers on adopting the challenger's model, the damage to the base business makes the answer no. Hastings put it this way in the foreword. "Rather than lacking vision, Hamilton established, these incumbents are in fact acting in an entirely predictable and economically rational way."

The trap is the "damned if you do, damned if you don't cul-de-sac." Adopt the new model and you cannibalize cash flows you still depend on. Ignore it and the challenger grows until those cash flows die anyway. He names three flavors of refusal. Milking, where the incumbent harvests the declining business rather than fund a new one. History's slave, where the organization cannot see why a model so unlike what built it would work. Job security, where the deciders have careers tied to the old model. On top of the economics, cognitive bias is almost always present. The typical response runs "Denial, Ridicule, Fear, Anger, Capitulation," and capitulation is frequently too late.

Counter-positioning is not disruptive technology, and he draws the line with two cases. In-N-Out against McDonald's is counter-positioning with no new technology. Kodak against digital photography is disruptive technology without counter-positioning. "Kodak's business model was built on its Power in film. It was not a camera company. Digital photography simply was not an attractive business opportunity for the company."

How a large company can play both sides. First, do the calculation openly instead of by reflex. If adopting the new model is negative net present value for the whole company, say so and milk deliberately, with a date. If the math is only negative because of how the base business is measured, the problem is structure, not economics, and the new model needs its own profit and loss judged against what a challenger would achieve. Second, if you will not build it, buy the challenger while it is cheap. Third, and rarest, cannibalize on purpose. Netflix killed its own red envelope business to move to streaming, and Shi notes people forget "how big of a shift it was." The challenger's test cuts the other way. "The only bet worthwhile for a challenger is one in which even if the incumbent plays its best game, it can be taken off the board." If the incumbent can answer without damage, you have a feature, not a power.

### Why well-run large companies stop compounding

Helmer starts from arbitrage. "Business is hard. Everyone is always trying to eat your lunch. The arbitrage is always there and withering." Power is a refuge from that, but every refuge has a half-life. Kodak had branding, process power, and cornered resources in film chemistry, and a technology shift made all three irrelevant at once. A well-run company can do everything right inside a market whose power is decaying.

The progression explains the rest. Once a company's market reaches stability, the only powers it can still build are the slow ones. Growth from here comes two ways. Extend existing power into adjacent markets where the barrier still applies, which he calls finding where the power umbrella fits. Or invent a second business at origination, which is "a different muscle," and which he and Shi studied through AWS, Nintendo, and Nvidia's CUDA. Their finding is that the second business should start from an existing advantage and must solve the power question as well as the product question. "You have to solve the power equation or else you end up competing in a commodity way on your base business." He also names the tension. "You have this balance, which is really hard. Show a commitment to doing new things, but also show a commitment to doing your usual thing really well."

On investing, he separates a great business from a great investment. Power is what makes cash flows persist. Price is what the market already believes about that persistence. The return comes from the gap, most often because the market misjudges duration. So he reads a business as a set of powers with expiry dates rather than a set of metrics. He admires Buffett and calls him a genius, but says the word moat "makes you think about the barrier piece" only, while power insists on the benefit too. "The Seven Powers are, for me, a very careful, and I hope, exhaustive articulation of the nature of moats."

## Teaching stories

Netflix vs Blockbuster. Netflix launched DVD by mail with no late fees. Blockbuster reported about 800 million dollars in late fees in 2000, roughly 16 percent of revenue and a much larger share of profit. Matching Netflix meant giving up the fees and stranding the store base, so Blockbuster waited, then answered late with a hybrid that hurt its own economics. The incumbent had more money, more customers, and more brand, and none of it helped. Helmer's second Netflix story is the originals decision, which turned licensed content from a variable cost into a fixed one. Hastings told him that if the networks stopped selling shows, "the company has a game plan. We just do more originals."

Intel. The same founders, Noyce, Moore, and Grove, ran a memory business with no power and a microprocessor business with three. In memory, Japanese producers matched everything Intel did. In microprocessors, the IBM PC design win and Operation Crush during the 1975 to 1983 takeoff built scale economies, network economies through the software base, and switching costs in one stretch. Helmer's lesson is that talent is not power. The team was constant. The power came from the market and the timing.

Kodak and the digital camera. Kodak invented the digital camera in 1975. Its power lived in film, chemistry, a brand, and a processing network built over decades. Digital photography offered none of those benefits and destroyed all of them. Helmer files this under disruptive technology, not counter-positioning, and uses it to show that power decays when the market moves, no matter how well the company is run.

Pixar. The Brain Trust of Ed Catmull, John Lasseter, and Steve Jobs, plus the early group that made the first Toy Story films and A Bug's Life, is Helmer's cornered resource. Nobody had arbitraged it away, it kept producing, and it was sufficient to explain the box office. The barrier was that the people chose to stay.

In-N-Out. A limited menu, fresh ingredients, higher pay, no franchising, slow expansion. McDonald's could have copied any of it and copied none of it, because every element would have damaged a business built on franchise growth and menu breadth. Counter-positioning needs no new technology, only a model the incumbent's own economics forbid.

Tiffany. Same diamond, twice the price, because of the blue box and a century of reinforcing actions. Branding is a real power with a real barrier, time, and it belongs to the stability stage. Every product that drifts from the image spends the asset.

Toyota. The Toyota Production System was documented, taught, and toured. General Motors saw it up close through the NUMMI joint venture and still took years to approach it. "Even Toyota did not have full, top-down understanding of what they had created. It took fifteen years to transfer TPS to suppliers." That opacity is the barrier.

Adobe. Helmer consulted for Adobe for years, and Bruce Chizen, its former CEO, endorsed the book. His written use of Adobe is Acrobat, the capabilities-led path to compelling value. Adobe had the technology before it had a customer who wanted a portable document, and kept searching until the need appeared. A capability with no known need is the riskiest path.

Facebook and network economies. The book uses LinkedIn against BranchOut, a startup that built a professional network on Facebook's social graph. It reached tens of millions of users and collapsed, because the network for a job search is not the network for friends. Facebook's power was real and bounded. With Shi he extends this to platforms in general. Two-sided growth is a sign of product-market fit, and multihoming can strip the power out of it entirely.

Vanguard vs Fidelity. Bogle launched the first index fund in 1976. Fidelity could have offered one any time it liked. It did not, because promoting a fund with fees a fraction of active management would have told its own customers to stop paying for active management. Ned Johnson of Fidelity said he could not believe the mass of investors would be satisfied with average returns. Rational on the day, ruinous over the horizon.

## His voice

Helmer sounds like an economist who has spent thirty years in boardrooms and has no patience left for adjectives. He uses defined terms and capitalizes them. He asks for the benefit in cash-flow units and the barrier as a mechanism. He distrusts words like differentiation, execution, culture, and vision unless they cash out into one of the seven. He prefers a single clear example to a survey, and he does not mind saying "that's wrong, actually" about his own earlier view.

## Questions he would ask about a large, optimized consumer platform

The situation. A few thousand people, a strong brand, a large installed base, a fixed royalty cost structure that caps gross margin, and a leadership team that has already squeezed the operating model. Helmer would not ask about growth levers. He would ask about power, market, and stage.

1. Which of the seven powers does this business hold today, and what is the cash-flow benefit of each, in units? Not "we have a great brand." How much price or retention does the brand buy relative to an identical product?
2. For each power, what is the barrier mechanism, and what is its half-life? What would a well-run competitor with unlimited money have to give up to take it?
3. Is the royalty structure a cornered resource, a switching cost, or a tax? If the catalog is licensed on the same terms to every competitor, it is a tax, and no amount of scale on top of it creates power.
4. What stage is the core market in? If it is in stability, which powers are still open, and are you building branding or process power on purpose, or just operating well?
5. Where does the market size term move? Optimizing the existing business changes power at the margin. A 10x comes from a larger market, a new market, or a changed cost structure. Which of the three are you pursuing?
6. What is the second business? Start from the advantage you already hold. What does the installed base, the brand, or the data let you enter at origination that nobody else can enter on the same terms?
7. Who is counter-positioned against you right now? Which business model would hurt you to copy, and are you at denial, ridicule, fear, or anger?
8. Who could you counter-position against? Which incumbent in an adjacent market has a cost structure or channel that forbids matching a move that is cheap for you?
9. If you moved the royalty cost from variable to fixed, as Netflix did with originals, what would that do to the barrier? What would the suppliers do in response, and could they do it in time?
10. What would you have to stop doing? Name the products, deals, or metrics that exist to protect the base business's margin and that make the new model unfundable inside the company.
11. Is the growth loop you describe evidence of power, or of product-market fit? Where does multihoming take the value back out?
12. What does the market not believe that you know? If investors already price the current power as permanent, more of the same adds nothing.

## How he would think about a 10x to 100x drop in the cost of building software

This section applies the framework. Helmer discussed AI's effect on barriers to entry on Lenny's Podcast in 2024, but the reasoning below is inference from his published work, not sourced quotes.

Start from the equation. A collapse in build cost mostly moves the market size term, because software becomes worth building for problems that never justified an engineering team. It moves the power term the other way for any business whose barrier was the cost of building. So the first question is not "what can we build now" but "what stops everyone else from building the same thing."

Powers that weaken. Process power in software organizations weakens first. The barrier was opacity and the years it took to accumulate embedded activity sets. When an agent can re-derive a pipeline in a week, hysteresis shrinks. Engineering scale economies weaken next. A fixed development cost spread over a large user base was a real barrier when the fixed cost was large. If it drops 100x for everyone, the leader's advantage drops with it. Procedural switching costs weaken too, since an agent can now do the migration for the customer. Any benefit that reduces to "we can afford to build more than they can" is now operational excellence.

Powers that strengthen. Network economies are untouched, because the barrier is other people, not code. A competitor can clone the product in a day and still face an empty room. Cornered resources in data, licensed catalog, distribution rights, and regulatory position strengthen, because when the product is free to copy, the input that cannot be copied carries the whole barrier. Branding strengthens when the market floods with near-identical products, since "historical information about the seller" is what a buyer reaches for when offerings look the same. Scale economies survive in compute and data, but only for the largest players.

Where counter-positioning opens. Cheap software means cheap experiments with business models, and business models are what incumbents cannot copy. Look for incumbents whose cost structure is fixed by contract or by headcount. A company with a fixed royalty cost cannot adopt a model that hands more value to the customer, because the royalty does not move. A company with thousands of people cannot adopt a model whose economics assume fifty. A company that sells seats cannot adopt a model where agents replace seats. Each is a "damned if you do" trap a challenger can walk into on purpose. The test is unchanged. Would the incumbent, playing its best game, still refuse to match?

For a large company with a strong platform and brand, this cuts both ways. Its process power and engineering scale are worth less. Its brand, installed base, and proprietary data are worth more. Put the cheap build cost behind the powers that still hold. Use the brand and the base to enter adjacent markets at origination. Convert data and rights into cornered resources with contracts, not assumptions. And run the counter-positioning audit on yourself first, because the cost drop makes every fixed line in your own cost structure a target.
