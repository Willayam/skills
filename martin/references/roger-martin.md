# Roger L. Martin reference

Source material for the `martin` advisor skill. Quotes are verified against the cited source. Where a story comes from a secondary summary rather than Martin's own text, the entry says so.

## Who he is

Roger Martin was born in 1956 in Wallenstein, Ontario, and took an AB and an MBA at Harvard. He spent thirteen years at Monitor Company, the consultancy Michael Porter co-founded, where he rose to director, founded the Canadian office, and co-headed the firm for two years. Clients included Procter & Gamble and Lego. He then served as dean of the Rotman School of Management at the University of Toronto from 1998 to 2013 and led the Martin Prosperity Institute. Thinkers50 ranked him the number one management thinker in the world in 2017. He was appointed to the Order of Canada in 2016 and sits on the boards of Thomson Reuters, the Skoll Foundation and Tennis Canada.

His books trace one argument from several angles. The Opposable Mind, 2007, is about integrative thinking. The Design of Business, 2009, is about the knowledge funnel and the fight between reliability and validity. Playing to Win, 2013 with A.G. Lafley, is the strategy method they used at P&G. When More Is Not Better, 2020, applies the same instinct against over-optimization to the whole economy. A New Way to Think, 2022, collects his HBR pieces on management models that fail in practice. Since 2020 he has published a weekly Playing to Win/Practitioner Insights piece on Medium, more than 250 by early 2026, which is where his sharpest current thinking lives.

## Core frameworks

### The strategy choice cascade

Strategy is five questions answered as one integrated set. What is our winning aspiration? Where will we play? How will we win? What capabilities must be in place? What management systems are required? He draws them as a cascade because each answer constrains the next and each lower answer feeds back to test the ones above. A where-to-play that your capabilities cannot serve is not a choice, it is a wish. A how-to-win that no management system measures will quietly revert to whatever the company did before.

His definition, revised in 2022, is "an integrated set of choices that compels desired customer action." The older 1998 version talked about positioning in an industry and sustainable advantage. He stripped it down to the one thing a company does not control, which is whether the customer buys. Everything else in the cascade exists to make that happen.

A winning aspiration is a statement about winning with specific customers, not a financial target and not a mission statement. Olay's aspiration at P&G was market share leadership in North America, a billion dollars in sales, and a global share among the leaders. Note the word winning. He is blunt that firms who set out to participate rather than win "will inevitably fail to make the tough choices." A winning aspiration is not "be the best" without a field, and it is not "grow 20 percent," which says nothing about whom you serve or why they would choose you.

How to run it. Write all five answers on one page. Then check the pairs. Does the how-to-win only work on this where-to-play? Would a rival find this where-to-play obviously stupid, or obviously smart? If everyone would make the same choice, it is an operating imperative, not strategy. Then ask which capabilities are must-haves for the how-to-win, and which existing management systems would fight the new choices.

### Strategy is choice, planning is not strategy

The Big Lie of Strategic Planning, HBR, January 2014, is his most cited essay. The argument is psychological before it is analytical. Executives find strategy frightening because it means guessing at a future and cutting off options. So they turn it into planning, which is comfortable because it deals with things the company controls. A lofty vision, a list of initiatives, and financials that dovetail with the budget. "Planning typically isn't explicit about what the organization chooses not to do and why. It does not question assumptions. And its dominant logic is affordability."

The deeper point is about revenue versus cost. Costs are under the company's control, so cost planning works. Revenue is decided by customers, who "can decide of their own free will whether to give revenue to the company, to its competitors, or to no one at all." Managers apply cost-planning habits to revenue and are then baffled when the revenue does not appear.

A plan is a list of initiatives with budgets. A strategy is a bet with a theory of winning, and the theory must be written down so you can see when it breaks. His three rules. Keep the strategy statement to one page built around where to play and how to win. Accept that strategy is not about perfection, only about shortening the odds. Make the logic explicit. "For your choices to make sense, what do you need to believe about customers, about the evolution of your industry, about competition, about your capabilities?" His warning sign is comfort itself. "If you are entirely comfortable with your strategy, there's a strong chance it isn't very good."

In the ReD Associates interview he says it plainer. "The essence of strategy is to compel the thing you do not control to do what you need it to do."

### What would have to be true

This is his most practical tool and the one he says is the most valuable question in strategy. The process, which he calls strategic choice structuring, runs in steps.

1. Frame the problem as a choice between at least two options, not as an issue to study.
2. Generate possibilities. Aim for several, including ones the room considers unlikely. He asks teams to write each as a happy story, a short narrative of how the world looks if the possibility succeeds.
3. For each possibility, specify what would have to be true for it to be a great choice. Conditions about customers, the industry, competitors, and your own capabilities and costs. Not what is true, what would have to be.
4. Identify the barrier conditions. The ones the team is least confident hold. The rule is that the person most skeptical of a condition gets to design the test for it.
5. Design and run tests, cheapest and most decisive first.
6. Choose the possibility whose conditions are either true now or can plausibly be made true.

Why this beats debating opinions. In a normal strategy meeting, people argue about what is true and the most senior or most confident voice wins. Asking what would have to be true lets a skeptic engage with an idea without endorsing it. It "turns the management dialogue from arguing about evaluating today to imagining winning futures." Nobody has to be wrong in public. Afterward the conditions become the monitoring dashboard. Arrive at your desk each morning and ask whether they still hold.

His design-thinking addition, from the 2020 Medium piece Strategy and Design Thinking, is the important twist. "We shouldn't automatically choose the strategy possibility for which all elements of the WWHTBT currently hold true. Instead, a superior possibility may be one for which one or more key elements are currently not true, but we develop a transformation plan that is both plausible and compelling for making them true." The false condition is the opportunity, because if it were already true, a competitor would already be doing it.

### Integrative thinking and the opposable mind

The Opposable Mind defines integrative thinking as "the ability to face constructively the tension of opposing ideas and, instead of choosing one at the expense of the other, generate a creative resolution of the tension in the form of a new idea that contains elements of the opposing ideas but is superior to each."

He built the idea from interviews with leaders like Isadore Sharp of Four Seasons and A.G. Lafley. The conventional thinker accepts the world as it is and picks the least bad option from a tradeoff. The integrative thinker treats the tradeoff as a sign that the problem is badly framed. "If you can define the problem differently than everybody else in the industry, you can generate alternatives that others aren't thinking about."

How to run it. State both models fully and fairly, especially the one you dislike. Ask what each gets right that the other cannot. Look for the causal relationships each model ignores. Design a third model that keeps the best of both. The test is that neither original camp would call the result a compromise.

### The knowledge funnel

The Design of Business describes how value creation moves through three stages. A mystery, a question nobody knows how to answer. A heuristic, a rule of thumb that narrows the search enough for skilled people to work on it. An algorithm, a fixed formula anyone can run. McDonald's turned the mystery of what Americans want to eat into a heuristic about fast, cheap food and then into an algorithm a teenager can execute.

The problem is what happens after. Companies that have converted everything to algorithm optimize for reliability, meaning consistent, predictable outcomes. Value comes from validity, meaning outcomes that meet the objective. "Companies that devote all their resources to reliability lack the tools to pursue outcomes that are valid." Every management system rewards the algorithm because it is measurable and the mystery is not. Exploitation crowds out exploration through the ordinary operation of budgets, metrics and promotions, with no one deciding it. His prescription is to run both and judge the exploration side on different terms. "The velocity of movement through the knowledge funnel, powered by design thinking, is the most powerful formula for competitive advantage in the twenty-first century."

### When more is not better

The 2020 book takes the reliability argument to the whole economy. Since Adam Smith, economics has treated efficiency as an unqualified good. Martin's claim is that efficiency past a point produces a fragile system with a Pareto distribution of outcomes instead of a healthy Gaussian one. Two things push a bell curve toward a power law. Increasing pressure on every part, and decreasing the cost of connection between parts. When "effects become the cause of more of the given effect," you get a long tail. Instagram followers behave this way. Adult height does not.

"But instead of a machine, the economy is a complex adaptive system. You can't break it into chunks and hope to add it back up." Hospitals that cut nursing slack and PPE stock to the efficient minimum were his 2020 example. His fix is three balances. Pressure with friction. Perfection with continual improvement. Connection with separation. Excess stock in a storeroom is friction, and friction buys resilience. For a company, a fully optimized system has no capacity to absorb a shock or to try something new. Slack is not waste. It is the reserve that lets a system last.

### Where to play

His rule is that a where-to-play choice must be one a sensible competitor could reject. "Everywhere" fails that test. The Big Lie has a sidebar on giant opportunities. "When anyone could be a customer, it is impossible to figure out whom to target and what those people actually want. The results tend to be an offering that is not captivating to anybody and a sales force that doesn't know where to spend its time." His advice for a huge market is sequential. Pick one piece, win it, then move to the next.

Customers, not competitors, define the field. Where to play is a choice about which customers, which needs, which channels, which geographies, which stages of production. It is not a choice about which rivals to fight. He is dismissive of strategy built by watching the competition. "Simply following competitors' choices will never produce a unique or valuable advantage." Where to play and how to win are inseparable. A distinctive where-to-play enables a how-to-win that would be impossible on the broad field. Southwest chose short-haul flights between secondary airports with one class of service, and that choice is what made the lowest cost position possible.

### The critique of customer-centric and data-driven strategy

The test is whether the opposite of your choice is stupid on its face. "The opposite of the choice to be customer-centric is to ignore customers entirely, which is stupid on its face." So customer-centricity is an operating imperative, something every competitor must and will do, and it generates no advantage. "It is only a strategic choice to be customer-centric in a way that is distinctive from the way competitors are being customer-centric."

On data, his position is that analysis of the past cannot produce a future that differs from the past. "You can't analyze your way to great strategy," because "analysis simply affirms the past." In domains where the future can differ from the past, and strategy is such a domain, the job is to imagine possibilities, pick the one with the most compelling argument, then test it. He is not anti-data. He wants data used to test conditions that imagination generated, not to generate the conditions. His Medium piece How to Prevent Data Analytics from Wrecking Your Strategy argues that a firm run by analytics will believe the future is an extrapolation of the past, and will then be right, because it will act to make it so.

### Strategy as iterative prototyping

The 2014 HBR piece starts from the impossibility of prediction. "Managers have no way of predicting with any certainty what will happen with respect to an industry and its likely evolution, customers and their likely preferences, a firm itself and its potential capabilities and cost structure, and competitors and their likely responses/actions. We just can't do it." His answer is to treat a strategy as a prototype rather than a plan. Build a rough version, put it in front of real customers, learn, revise. He later wrote that "the process of rapid, iterative prototyping was in fact a process for making something true that didn't start out that way."

The practical test is the happy customer. Before scaling anything, produce one customer who is delighted by the offering under the new choices, then a handful, then a segment. If you cannot make one customer happy at prototype scale, no amount of planning will produce a segment of them. The prototype is also the cheapest test of the barrier conditions from the what-would-have-to-be-true list.

## Teaching stories

Olay. In the late 1990s Oil of Olay was an aging brand selling a pink cream at about four dollars in drugstores to women over fifty. The team refused the obvious choices, milk it or take it to prestige channels where P&G had no capabilities. They created a new where-to-play, women 35 to 50 worried about the first signs of aging, and a new how-to-win, a prestige-quality product at a masstige price near 19 dollars sold in mass retail with a prestige-like shelf experience. What had to be true was that mass retailers would give premium shelf space and that a mass shopper would pay that price. They tested price points and found 18.99 worked where 15.99 did not. Olay grew to a multibillion-dollar brand.

Swiffer and Febreze. Both came out of Lafley's Connect and Develop program, which set a target that half of P&G's innovation would involve outside partners. The strategic choice was a where-to-play in innovation itself. Swiffer created a category rather than fighting in an existing one, and it illustrates his point that the most valuable where-to-play is often a need customers had not named.

Southwest. The where-to-play was short-haul, point-to-point flights between secondary airports with a single class. The how-to-win was the lowest cost per seat mile in the industry, through one aircraft type, fast turnarounds and no meals. He uses it to show inseparability. The cost position only exists because of the narrow field, and a full-service airline cannot copy it without abandoning its own.

Vanguard. Jack Bogle chose to offer only index funds. The opposite choice, active management, was not stupid on its face, which is what made it a real strategic choice. The cost advantage came from not doing something. Others sell index funds now, but Vanguard's head start in scale is still unmatched. He calls faux low-cost strategies, where the choices are ones everyone would make, a margin problem waiting to happen.

Four Seasons. Isadore Sharp faced an industry tradeoff between small motels with warmth and no amenities, and large hotels with amenities and no warmth. He refused it and built medium-sized hotels big enough for full amenities and small enough for personal service, redefining luxury as replacing what the guest left at home and office. Martin uses Sharp as a model integrative thinker. He tested the model with one hotel, the Inn on the Park in London, before expanding.

Lego. Martin advised Lego through Monitor and Jorgen Vig Knudstorp endorsed Playing to Win. The story, told in secondary summaries rather than Martin's own text, is that Lego near bankruptcy in 2003 narrowed its where-to-play back to the brick system and family play, cut sprawling adjacencies, and built capabilities in licensing and retail. A firm can grow by choosing less.

Apple. In his Medium series he notes that one of the first things Jobs did on returning was cancel the broad product lines, and that Apple widened its where-to-play only after it had won the narrower one. Win a field, then widen it.

Crisp, contrarian, and impatient with the machinery of corporate strategy. He writes in first person, names the thing he thinks is wrong, and gives a test you can apply in the next meeting. He likes the word "stupid." He does not soften.

## Questions he would ask about growth in a large, optimized consumer platform

- What is the winning aspiration, stated in terms of customers rather than revenue? Whom do you intend to win with, and against what alternative?
- Where do you play today, in words a competitor could disagree with? If the honest answer is "everyone who uses the product," you have no where-to-play choice, and that is the first problem.
- What is the how-to-win, and is the opposite of it stupid on its face? "Great product, great brand, great data" fails the test. Every serious rival claims the same.
- Which of your current choices would you have to reverse for the business to double? Not add, reverse. What would you stop?
- Your optimization work assumes the customer's job is fixed and only your delivery of it can improve. What is the customer trying to do that your product still forces them to do somewhere else?
- Which parts of the business have been converted fully to algorithm, and where is the last live heuristic? Who in the company is still working on a mystery, and what does the management system do to them?
- Write down three possibilities for a 10x outcome. For each, what would have to be true about customers, about the industry, about competitors and about your capabilities? Which condition do you believe least?
- Who is the skeptic on each of those conditions, and what test would satisfy them at a cost you could afford to lose?
- Is the strategy being measured by customer metrics or by cost and capability metrics? If the latter, the board is supervising a plan, not a strategy.
- What is the last thing this company chose not to do, on purpose, and said so?

## Applying the cascade when building software gets 10x to 100x cheaper

His starting move would be to reframe the change. A 10x drop in the cost of building is a change in the cost side, which is the side the company controls. It is not a change in demand, and it is not by itself a strategy. Every competitor gets the same cheaper building. So the first question is not "what can we now build" but "what can we now compel customers to do that they would not do before, and that a rival cannot easily match."

Cheaper building makes choice more important, not less, for three reasons. First, when building is expensive, the budget does the choosing. Scarcity forces a where-to-play by accident. When building is nearly free, nothing external stops the company from shipping everything, and "everything" is the non-choice that produces an offering "not captivating to anybody." A company that never learned to choose will drown in its own roadmap. Second, if everyone can build, features become operating imperatives. The opposite of "ship fast with AI" is stupid on its face, so shipping fast with AI is not a strategy. Advantage moves to the choices that stay hard to copy, which are where-to-play, the customer relationship, the brand, distribution, and the willingness to stop doing things. Third, cheap building lowers the cost of prototypes, so the what-would-have-to-be-true process gets better. Conditions that took a quarter and a team to test can be tested in a week by one person. The firm that runs the most tests against the best-imagined conditions wins. But "LLM/AI will find the mean," so imagining the possibilities stays a human job.

He would then run the cascade.

Winning aspiration. Restate it around customer outcomes that only become possible at the new cost of building. If the company could run a hundred bets where it used to run five, what would winning look like for a customer, not for the P&L?

Where to play. Two moves open up. One is depth. Segments too small to justify a dedicated product can now have one, and a platform with a strong brand can serve many narrow needs under one trusted name where a startup can pick only one. The other is adjacency the customer already wants. The Olay move, taking a trusted brand into a need the customer solves elsewhere, gets cheaper to try. His warning is to sequence. Pick one, win it, then the next. A hundred half-won segments is the giant-opportunity trap at higher speed.

How to win. When everyone can build the feature, the how-to-win cannot be the feature. It has to be a distinctive way of being right about customers faster than rivals. The rate of prototype learning, data no rival has, brand permission to enter new needs, and the discipline to kill what fails. "We use AI to build faster" fails the opposite-stupid test. "We alone run live experiments with five million users who trust the brand, and we kill 80 percent within a month" might pass.

Capabilities. The must-haves shift from engineering headcount to judgment. Framing choices, writing conditions, designing cheap decisive tests, reading results honestly. He wrote in 2024 that AI will replace strategy work that seeks the common answer and cannot replace work that seeks the unique one. The needed capability is people who have formalized their own heuristics well enough to know which is which.

Management systems. This is where he would predict failure. An optimized company's systems reward reliability. They will treat a hundred cheap prototypes as a hundred cost lines and demand a business case for each, which reintroduces the old cost of building through process. He would insist on a separate system that judges exploration on validity, with its own metrics and budget, and treats slack as a reserve rather than waste. Otherwise cheaper building only makes the algorithm run faster.

His likely closing line would be a question. If building is nearly free, what would have to be true for the company to choose to build less?
