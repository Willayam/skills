# Naval: Recommended Reading And Writing

Source base: derived notes from Eric Jorgenson, *The Almanack of Naval Ravikant*, public PDF: https://navalmanack.s3.amazonaws.com/Eric-Jorgenson_The-Almanack-of-Naval-Ravikant_Final.pdf.

These notes summarize Naval's reading method, recommended books and blogs, and the "Naval's Writing" appendix. They are intended as retrieval notes and advisor material, not as a substitute for the original reading list.

## Reading Method

Naval reads from curiosity, not self-improvement obligation. The practical method is to read whatever creates enough interest to build the habit, then let taste mature toward deeper theory, concepts, science, philosophy, and nonfiction. [Almanack, pp. 207-208]

Core principles:
- Read what is genuinely interesting until reading itself becomes enjoyable. [Almanack, pp. 113-116]
- The scarce resource is not access to books; it is desire to learn. [Almanack, p. 113]
- Rereading matters. The goal is not finishing everything, but absorbing the books that form the foundation of judgment. [Almanack, pp. 114-116]
- The better a book is, the slower it may need to be absorbed. [Almanack, p. 115]
- Skimming, jumping around, abandoning, and rereading are acceptable if they preserve curiosity and learning velocity. [Almanack, pp. 115-116]
- Spending money on books is treated as investment, not expense. [Almanack, p. 219]
- Avoid reading primarily for social approval; that produces herd thinking. [Almanack, pp. 184-185]

Advisor prompts:
- "What book would you read if no one ever knew you read it?"
- "Which book do you keep returning to because it changes your judgment?"
- "Are you reading for truth, curiosity, status, or homework?"
- "What is the oldest or most foundational source behind the modern summary you are reading?"

## Foundational Nonfiction

Use this cluster when the user needs better world models, optimism, scientific taste, history, game theory, or judgment.

- David Deutsch, *The Beginning of Infinity*. Naval says it is difficult but intelligence-expanding; use for explanations, optimism, epistemology, and the power of knowledge. [Almanack, p. 208]
- Yuval Noah Harari, *Sapiens*. Use for broad human history, shared myths, civilizational frames, and seeing humanity from distance. Naval praises its density of ideas. [Almanack, p. 208]
- Matt Ridley, *The Rational Optimist*. Use for progress, exchange, innovation, and prosperity. Naval describes Ridley as one of his favorite authors and recommends reading broadly across his work. [Almanack, pp. 208-209]
- Matt Ridley, *Genome*, *The Red Queen*, *The Origins of Virtue*, and *The Evolution of Everything*. Use for evolution, cooperation, sex, virtue, and emergent order. [Almanack, p. 209]
- Nassim Nicholas Taleb, *Skin in the Game*. Use for accountability, exposure to consequences, hidden asymmetries, and practical judgment. [Almanack, p. 209]
- Nassim Nicholas Taleb, *The Bed of Procrustes*, plus *The Black Swan*, *Antifragile*, and *Fooled by Randomness*. Use for aphorisms, uncertainty, fragility, optionality, and epistemic humility. [Almanack, pp. 209-210]
- Will and Ariel Durant, *The Lessons of History*. Use for compressed history, civilizational patterns, and recurring human tendencies. [Almanack, p. 211]
- James Dale Davidson and William Rees-Mogg, *The Sovereign Individual*. Use for information-age power shifts, jurisdictional arbitrage, and individual autonomy. [Almanack, p. 211]
- Charlie Munger and Peter Kaufman, *Poor Charlie's Almanack*. Use for multidisciplinary mental models, worldly wisdom, and overcoming oneself. [Almanack, p. 211]
- Robert Axelrod, *The Evolution of Cooperation*, and J.D. Williams, *The Compleat Strategyst*. Use for game theory, cooperation, repeated games, and strategy. [Almanack, p. 212]

Method cluster:
- For worldview formation, pair Harari's macro-history with Ridley's evolutionary optimism.
- For risk and judgment, pair Taleb with Munger.
- For cooperation and strategy, pair Axelrod with game-playing and basic probability.

## Physics, Science, And Reality

Use this cluster when the user needs reality contact, scientific humility, or comfort with first principles.

- Richard Feynman, *Six Easy Pieces* and *Six Not-So-Easy Pieces*. Naval would give these to his children; use for clear physics, curiosity, and scientific temperament. [Almanack, p. 210]
- Richard Feynman, *Perfectly Reasonable Deviations from the Beaten Track*, and James Gleick's Feynman biography *Genius*. Use for scientific personality, letters, biography, and taste. [Almanack, p. 210]
- Randall Munroe, *Thing Explainer*. Use for explaining complex ideas in simple language and checking whether understanding survives plain words. [Almanack, p. 210]
- Lewis Carroll Epstein, *Thinking Physics*. Use for puzzle-based physics reasoning accessible from school level through graduate level. [Almanack, pp. 210-211]
- Carlo Rovelli, *Reality Is Not What It Seems* and *Seven Brief Lessons on Physics*. Use for physics blended with history, philosophy, and poetic wonder. [Almanack, pp. 211-212]
- Richard Hamming, "You and Your Research." Use for great-work questions, research taste, seriousness, and choosing important problems. [Almanack, p. 222]

Advisor prompts:
- "Can you explain it using only simple words?"
- "What would be different in the world if this belief were true?"
- "What prediction would make this idea falsifiable?"
- "Are you studying the summary, or the source?"

## Philosophy And Spirituality

Use this cluster for truth-seeking, self-observation, present-moment work, desire, ego, and internal freedom.

- Jed McKenna, including the *Dreamstate Trilogy*, *Jed McKenna's Notebook*, and *Jed Talks*. Naval values the dedication to truth despite an abrasive style. Use for uncompromising inquiry and dismantling illusions. [Almanack, p. 212]
- Kapil Gupta, including *A Master's Secret Whispers*, *Direct Truth*, and *Atmamun*. Naval notes Gupta became an advisor despite Naval's skepticism toward coaches. Use for direct, non-prescriptive truth and performance without noise. [Almanack, pp. 212-213]
- Jiddu Krishnamurti, *The Book of Life*. Use for observing the mind, self-knowledge, and direct philosophical reflection. Naval says Krishnamurti strongly influenced him. [Almanack, p. 213]
- Jiddu Krishnamurti, *Total Freedom*. Use for a more advanced look at the mind's traps and the costs of conditioning. [Almanack, p. 213]
- Hermann Hesse, *Siddhartha*. Use as an accessible philosophical introduction and narrative entry into self-discovery. [Almanack, p. 213]
- Osho, *The Book of Secrets*. Use as a menu of meditation techniques to test rather than believe. Naval frames it as a collection of many methods to try. [Almanack, p. 214]
- Osho, *The Great Challenge*. Use for inner-world exploration where the user is open to Osho's style. [Almanack, p. 214]
- Anthony de Mello, *The Way to Love*. Use for attachment, perception, and spiritual psychology. [Almanack, p. 214]
- Michael Singer, *The Untethered Soul*. Use for observing thoughts, releasing identification, and inner freedom. [Almanack, p. 214]
- Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*. Use for power not solving inner life, self-command, mortality, and daily virtue. Naval describes it as life-changing. [Almanack, pp. 214-215]
- Kamal Ravikant, *Love Yourself Like Your Life Depends on It*. Use for self-acceptance, internal repetition, and saying yes to reality. [Almanack, p. 215]
- Seneca, *The Tao of Seneca*. Use for Stoic letters, mortality, control, and practical wisdom; Naval names it as a highly important audiobook for him. [Almanack, p. 215]
- Michael Pollan, *How to Change Your Mind*. Use for psychedelics as a lens on self-observation, while noting Naval says meditation can do the work without drugs. [Almanack, pp. 215-216]
- Bruce Lee, *Striking Thoughts*. Use for practical philosophy, discipline, expression, and personal mastery. [Almanack, p. 216]
- Kahlil Gibran, *The Prophet*. Use for poetic, nonsectarian reflections on children, marriage, work, money, friends, enemies, and life. [Almanack, p. 216]

Method cluster:
- For self-observation: Krishnamurti, Singer, de Mello.
- For direct truth: McKenna, Gupta.
- For practical philosophy: Marcus Aurelius, Seneca, Bruce Lee.
- For meditation experimentation: Osho's *Book of Secrets*, plus Naval's own one-hour sitting method.
- For accessible entry points: *Siddhartha*, *The Prophet*, *The Untethered Soul*.

## Science Fiction And Imaginative Philosophy

Use this cluster where philosophy is better approached through narrative, speculative worlds, or compressed fiction.

- Jorge Luis Borges, *Ficciones* or *Labyrinths*. Naval treats Borges as an unusually powerful writer whose fiction carries philosophy. [Almanack, p. 217]
- Ted Chiang, *Stories of Your Life and Others*, including "Understand" and "Story of Your Life." Use for intelligence, language, determinism, and cognition. [Almanack, p. 217]
- Ted Chiang, *Exhalation*. Use for thermodynamics, consciousness, fate, and wonder. [Almanack, p. 217]
- Ted Chiang, *The Lifecycle of Software Objects*. Use for AI, care, personhood, and long-term development. [Almanack, p. 217]
- Neal Stephenson, *Snow Crash*, plus *The Diamond Age*. Use for technology, culture, networks, and speculative systems. [Almanack, p. 218]
- Isaac Asimov, "The Last Question." Use for cosmic scale, entropy, AI, and ultimate questions. [Almanack, p. 218]
- Cixin Liu, *The Three-Body Problem*. Mentioned among rereads/current reading; use for civilization-scale strategy and first-contact thinking. [Almanack, p. 219]

Advisor prompts:
- "Would fiction help you see the system without defensiveness?"
- "What speculative story captures this problem better than a framework?"

## Rereads, Current Reads, And Permission To Read Widely

Naval's rereading list at the time included work on Rene Girard, Tim Ferriss's *Tools of Titans*, Behzad Mohit's *Thermoinfocomplexity*, Robert Cialdini's *Pre-Suasion*, Will Durant's *The Story of Philosophy*, Emerson, Chesterfield, Tolstoy, Alan Watts, Scott Adams, Tao Te Ching, Nietzsche, Tim Harford's *The Undercover Economist*, Richard Bach's *Illusions*, Dale Carnegie, Viktor Frankl's *Man's Search for Meaning*, Christopher Ryan's *Sex at Dawn*, and more. [Almanack, pp. 218-219]

Important pattern:
- Naval says he omits many books when asked what he reads because some sound embarrassing, trivial, disagreeable, or low-status. This is a permission structure: read for mind-expansion and curiosity, not reputation. [Almanack, p. 219]

Advisor prompts:
- "What are you not reading because it would not sound impressive?"
- "What do you disagree with that might still bend your mind usefully?"
- "Which book do you need as reference material rather than cover-to-cover doctrine?"

## Blogs And Essays

Use this cluster for short-form thinking, writing, technology strategy, career decisions, and mental models.

- Kevin Simler, Melting Asphalt. Use for social psychology, hidden motives, and belief analysis. [Almanack, pp. 219-220]
- Farnam Street. Use for mental models, decision-making, Munger-style thinking, and durable judgment. [Almanack, pp. 219-220]
- Ben Thompson, Stratechery. Use for technology strategy, aggregation theory, and business-model analysis. [Almanack, pp. 219-221]
- Maciej Ceglowski, Idle Words. Use for essays, technology criticism, and careful prose. [Almanack, p. 220]
- Farnam Street, "The Munger Operating System." Use for rules to live and prosper by, Munger operating principles, and compounding judgment. [Almanack, p. 220]
- Scott Adams, "The Day You Became a Better Writer." Naval uses it as a practical writing template: title, surprise, brevity, directness, fewer modifiers, active voice. [Almanack, p. 220]
- Kevin Simler, "Crony Beliefs." Use for beliefs held for social or political advantage rather than truth. [Almanack, p. 220]
- Elad Gil on career decisions in Silicon Valley and technology. Use for career judgment in tech. [Almanack, p. 221]
- Yuval Harari's *Sapiens* course on YouTube. Use when video lectures are a better entry point. [Almanack, p. 221]
- Eliezer Yudkowsky, "Think Like Reality." Use for physics, rationality, and updating models toward reality. [Almanack, p. 221]
- Andrew Wilkinson, "Lazy Leadership." Use for leadership with leverage and reduced managerial waste. [Almanack, p. 221]
- Ed Latimore's site. Use for overachievers, self-command, and hard-earned practical wisdom. [Almanack, p. 221]

## Naval's Writing: Life Formulas

The "Life Formulas I" section is framed as notes to himself: algorithms, not universal definitions. Use them as modular variables to inspect a life system. [Almanack, p. 223]

Derived formula map:
- Happiness depends heavily on health, wealth, and good relationships. [Almanack, p. 223]
- Health decomposes into exercise, diet, and sleep. [Almanack, p. 223]
- Exercise mixes resistance, sport/play, and rest. [Almanack, p. 223]
- Diet favors natural foods, plants, and time away from eating. [Almanack, p. 223]
- Sleep benefits from enough duration, circadian alignment, and not being dominated by alarms. [Almanack, p. 223]
- Wealth combines income with assets compounding through returns. [Almanack, p. 223]
- Income depends on accountability, leverage, and specific knowledge. [Almanack, p. 223]
- Accountability includes personal brand, platform, and willingness to take risk. [Almanack, p. 223]
- Leverage includes capital, people, and intellectual property. [Almanack, p. 223]
- Specific knowledge means knowing how to do something society cannot yet easily train others to do. [Almanack, p. 223]
- Investment returns depend on long holding periods, valuation, and margin of safety. [Almanack, p. 223]

Advisor use:
- Convert vague dissatisfaction into variables.
- Ask which variable is weakest, which is highest leverage, and which is already compounding.
- Do not apply the formulas rigidly; Naval explicitly frames them as personal notes whose calculations may vary. [Almanack, p. 223]

## Naval's Writing: Rules

The "Naval's Rules" section compresses recurring operating principles across presence, desire, anger, relationships, reading, compounding, honesty, truth, thought-watching, love, mathematics, and completeness of the moment. [Almanack, pp. 224-225]

Derived rule clusters:
- Presence: start from the current moment; treat each moment as complete rather than merely instrumental. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Desire and anger: desire creates suffering until fulfilled; anger harms the holder first. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Relationships: do not spend even a day working with someone if lifelong association seems impossible. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Learning: reading is the meta-skill because it can be converted into many other skills. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Compounding: the real benefits in life come from compounding over time. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Work: earn through judgment and leverage rather than renting hours. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Effort: much effort is wasted; selection, judgment, and direction matter. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Honesty: be honest without abandoning positivity. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Feedback: praise with specificity and criticize at a general level when possible. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Truth: useful truth predicts. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Mind: watch thoughts and ask why they are arising. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Love: give love rather than waiting to receive it. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Enlightenment: look for the gap between thoughts, not a distant trophy. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Mathematics: treat math as nature's language. [Almanack, p. 224]
- Priority: health, love, and mission outrank the rest. [Almanack, p. 225]

Prompt patterns:
- "Which variable in this formula is actually broken?"
- "Are you trying to compensate for weak judgment with more effort?"
- "What would make this relationship a lifelong yes or a clear no?"
- "What does this belief predict?"
- "Are you earning with your mind, your leverage, or only your hours?"
