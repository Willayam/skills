# Wealth and Leverage

Use this reference when the user is evaluating career direction, business
models, leverage, wealth creation, long-term games, focus, judgment, luck, or
whether a path can compound.

Source note: These are derived notes from Eric Jorgenson, *The Almanack of Naval
Ravikant*, Part I, "Building Wealth," using the public PDF:
https://navalmanack.s3.amazonaws.com/Eric-Jorgenson_The-Almanack-of-Naval-Ravikant_Final.pdf.
Page references use the printed book pagination, e.g. `[Almanack, p. 30]`.

## Part I Map

- "Understand How Wealth Is Created" frames wealth as a learnable skill, not a
  reward for undirected labor. [Almanack, pp. 29-39]
- "Find and Build Specific Knowledge" explains why curiosity, authenticity, and
  hard-to-train skill produce pricing power. [Almanack, pp. 40-45]
- "Play Long-Term Games with Long-Term People" applies compound interest to
  relationships, reputation, knowledge, and capital. [Almanack, pp. 46-49]
- "Take on Accountability," "Build or Buy Equity," and "Find a Position of
  Leverage" define the machinery of non-linear upside. [Almanack, pp. 50-67]
- "Get Paid for Your Judgment," "Prioritize and Focus," "Find Work That Feels
  Like Play," "How to Get Lucky," and "Be Patient" complete the operating
  system. [Almanack, pp. 67-91]

## Core Thesis

Wealth creation is a positive-sum skill. It comes from giving society something
it wants, does not yet know how to get, and can receive at scale. Money is a
transfer mechanism. Status is a ranking game. Wealth is ownership of assets,
products, businesses, intellectual property, or capital that can keep producing
without a matching hour of labor. [Almanack, pp. 30-39]

The recipe is not "work harder." Hard work matters only after the direction is
right. First find what to work on, who to work with, and when the timing and
market need are right. Then combine specific knowledge, accountability,
ownership, leverage, and judgment over a long enough time horizon. [Almanack,
pp. 30-37, 66-69]

Advisor posture: do not ask only "How do we make more money?" Ask:

- What does society want here that it cannot yet get?
- What part of this can the user uniquely provide?
- What makes the output scale without matching additional hours?
- Where does the user own the upside?
- Which decision will compound for years?

## Wealth, Money, and Status

### Wealth

Wealth is productive ownership: factories, products, code, media, businesses,
capital, rentable assets, or intellectual property that can continue creating
value when the owner is not actively working. The advisor should translate
"income" questions into "asset" questions. [Almanack, pp. 37-39, 53-54]

Decision questions:

- What asset is being built?
- Who owns it?
- Does it produce while the user sleeps, rests, learns, sells, or works on
  something else?
- Does each additional customer require roughly the same effort, or does effort
  decouple from output?
- Is the user creating a future claim on value, or only collecting current
  wages?

### Money

Money is a social credit system for transferring past and future value. It
solves money problems and can buy material freedom, but it does not automatically
create calm, health, love, meaning, or wisdom. [Almanack, pp. 37-38, 89-91]

Advisor move: when the user treats money as the final goal, separate the
external problem from the internal problem. Ask what freedom the money is meant
to buy, what burn rate must be covered, and what desires may expand to consume
the gains. [Almanack, pp. 77-81, 89-91]

### Status

Status is a zero-sum hierarchy. Wealth creation is positive-sum when it gives
people more of what they want. Many attacks on wealth creation are status moves
in disguise. Do not confuse moral judgment about status games with analysis of
whether useful value is being created. [Almanack, pp. 72-74]

Traps:

- Seeking prestige instead of ownership.
- Attacking other builders instead of making something useful.
- Optimizing for being seen as smart, ethical, or high status rather than
  becoming useful, trustworthy, and effective.
- Choosing a path because it increases social rank while reducing freedom.

## The Wealth Creation Loop

1. **Pick a positive-sum arena.** Look for a market where new value can be
   created, not merely redistributed. [Almanack, pp. 31-32, 72-74]
2. **Find a long-term game.** Prefer industries, relationships, and knowledge
   domains where repetition, trust, and compounding matter. [Almanack, pp.
   46-49]
3. **Find long-term people.** Evaluate intelligence, energy, and integrity
   together. Integrity is the gating factor. [Almanack, pp. 32, 46-49, 84-87]
4. **Learn to build or sell.** Builders create the product or service; sellers
   distribute, persuade, recruit, raise capital, and communicate. A person who
   can do both has unusual range. [Almanack, pp. 32, 63-64]
5. **Develop specific knowledge.** Build on curiosity, obsession, technical or
   creative skill, and the part of the user's personality that is hard to copy.
   [Almanack, pp. 40-45, 55-57]
6. **Take accountability.** Put the user's name on the line so credibility,
   responsibility, equity, and leverage can attach to them. [Almanack, pp.
   50-52]
7. **Own equity.** Wages cap upside. Ownership allows non-linear returns.
   [Almanack, pp. 53-54]
8. **Add leverage.** Use capital, labor, code, media, brand, distribution, and
   tools to multiply judgment. [Almanack, pp. 55-67]
9. **Improve judgment.** In leveraged systems, small differences in decision
   quality create enormous differences in outcome. [Almanack, pp. 67-69, 93-99]
10. **Wait without counting.** Act quickly, then let markets, reputation,
    relationships, and products compound. [Almanack, pp. 87-91, 181-182]

## Productize Yourself

"Productize yourself" compresses the wealth system into two questions:

- **Yourself:** What is authentic, specific, and accountable to the user?
- **Productize:** How can that authentic capability be packaged, scaled, and
  multiplied through leverage? [Almanack, pp. 37-38]

Use this lens when a user is considering consulting, employment, content,
software, education, investing, services, or a startup.

Operating checklist:

- Name the user's authentic edge: skill, taste, domain access, curiosity,
  obsession, credibility, or lived experience.
- Identify the unit of value society wants from that edge.
- Turn the unit into a repeatable product, process, asset, or public body of
  work.
- Choose leverage: code, media, capital, labor, brand, community, distribution,
  or systems.
- Attach accountability: name, promise, track record, guarantees, public proof,
  or visible ownership.
- Capture upside: equity, royalties, subscriptions, licensing, revenue share,
  fund carry, options, or direct ownership.
- Keep refining the category until the user can plausibly become one of the best
  in the world at the narrowly defined game. [Almanack, pp. 37-45, 55-67]

Prompt pattern:

```text
What part of this is "yourself" and what part is "productize"? Name the authentic
edge, the scalable package, the leverage, the accountability, and the ownership.
```

## Long-Term Games

Compound interest is not only financial. It applies to reputation, knowledge,
relationships, distribution, brand, trust, and product quality. The user should
search for the small number of commitments that deserve years of repetition.
[Almanack, pp. 46-49]

Signals of a long-term game:

- Repeat interactions matter more than one-off extraction.
- Trust reduces negotiation cost over time.
- Reputation increases deal flow or opportunity quality.
- Knowledge accumulates instead of becoming obsolete immediately.
- Partners can make each other richer without keeping exact score.
- The user's contribution gets easier to distribute as credibility grows.
- The game rewards patience, ethics, and visible accountability. [Almanack, pp.
  46-49, 84-87]

Signals of a short-term game:

- The user must hide information to win.
- Every dollar gained feels taken from someone else's pocket.
- Relationships are disposable.
- Reputation risk is treated as externality.
- The upside requires urgency, pressure, secrecy, or artificial scarcity.
- The user is counting favors and losing patience. [Almanack, pp. 72-87]

Advisor move: if the user is optimizing a negotiation, ask whether the
relationship or market will repeat. If it repeats, optimize for the next decade,
not the next dollar. [Almanack, pp. 46-49, 84-85]

## Leverage

Leverage multiplies judgment. Without leverage, the user is paid roughly for
inputs. With leverage, the same decision, code path, product, investment,
communication, or hire can produce thousands of times more output. [Almanack,
pp. 55-69]

### Labor

Labor leverage is people working for or with the user. It is ancient,
permissioned, and coordination-heavy. Someone must agree to follow. It can be
powerful but brings management, politics, culture, morale, and incentive
problems. [Almanack, pp. 34, 58]

Use labor leverage when:

- Human judgment, taste, trust, craft, or coordination is the bottleneck.
- The user has earned followership.
- The work cannot yet be productized or automated.
- The team increases quality and speed more than it increases complexity.

Avoid labor leverage when:

- Hiring is used as status.
- The user wants to avoid doing hard thinking.
- The team exists to compensate for unclear product direction.
- Management overhead consumes the creative or strategic work.

### Capital

Capital leverage is money, machines, assets, inventory, or investment capacity
working through the user's decisions. It is powerful but usually permissioned:
someone must entrust the user with capital or the user must have accumulated it.
[Almanack, pp. 34, 58]

Use capital leverage when:

- The user's judgment can allocate money better than alternatives.
- The opportunity is capital constrained, not idea constrained.
- Downside is controlled and risk of ruin is avoided.
- The user has a credible track record or a uniquely informed view.

Advisor move: ask whether the user's edge survives after capital arrives. Many
plans confuse "more money" with "better judgment." [Almanack, pp. 66-69]

### Code and Media

Code and media are permissionless leverage. They can be created by an individual
or small team, distributed globally, and replicated at near-zero marginal cost.
Software, writing, podcasts, video, public research, courses, protocols, and
tools are modern leverage forms. [Almanack, pp. 35, 58-60]

Use code and media when:

- The user can encode judgment, taste, or knowledge into a reusable artifact.
- Distribution can compound through search, networks, communities, or product
  loops.
- Replication cost is low relative to creation cost.
- The user's credibility increases as the artifact spreads.

Prompt pattern:

```text
If this had to work while you slept, would it be code, media, capital, labor, or
some combination? What would be created once and reused many times?
```

### Brand and Distribution

Brand is not listed as a separate leverage class in the main triad, but Part I
repeatedly treats reputation, accountability, and public work as opportunity
attractors. A trusted public identity can cause deals, talent, and lucky breaks
to route through the user. [Almanack, pp. 50-52, 57, 84-85]

Use brand as leverage only when it is backed by real judgment, integrity, and
work. A hollow brand increases fragility.

## Judgment

Judgment is wisdom applied to external decisions. In high-leverage contexts, a
small improvement in judgment can be worth vastly more than additional hours.
The highest-paid people are often paid for demonstrated judgment under
accountability, not for visible busyness. [Almanack, pp. 67-69, 93-95]

Advisor interpretation:

- Direction matters more than speed.
- The more leverage involved, the more expensive bad judgment becomes.
- A correct decision can create returns for years.
- A wrong decision with leverage can destroy time, capital, trust, or health.
- Good judgment requires experience, but foundational learning accelerates it.
  [Almanack, pp. 35-36, 67-69, 93-99]

Judgment checklist:

- What are the long-term consequences of this action?
- What would happen if this decision were repeated for ten years?
- What facts does the user not want to face?
- What desire is distorting the user's perception?
- What would a calm third party see immediately?
- Which foundation is weak: economics, psychology, game theory, persuasion,
  ethics, math, computers, or domain knowledge?
- Is the user optimizing for truth or identity?
- Does the plan need more effort, or a better direction? [Almanack, pp. 35-36,
  93-99]

Advisor move: insist on empty space for thinking. If the user's calendar has no
room for reflection, they are unlikely to make high-quality decisions.
[Almanack, pp. 36, 98]

## Prioritization and Focus

Opportunity abundance creates a time bottleneck. The user should not treat every
available path as equally real. The task is to free enough time and attention to
solve the few decisions and projects that dominate the curve. [Almanack, pp.
69-75]

### Personal Hourly Rate

Naval recommends an aspirational hourly rate high enough to feel almost
unreasonable, then using it to decide what to ignore, outsource, automate, or
buy. The point is not vanity. It is training the user's behavior to respect the
future value of their time. [Almanack, pp. 36, 69-72]

Use the hourly-rate filter:

- If a task costs less to delegate than the user's rate, delegate it.
- If a problem saves less than the rate, consider ignoring it.
- If a meeting does not create enough value, decline it.
- If a trip, errand, or argument consumes scarce attention, price it honestly.
- If the user's calendar is full of low-value obligations, their strategy is not
  real. [Almanack, pp. 36, 69-72]

Advisor caution: the rate is a prioritization tool, not a license to treat other
people poorly. Apply it to the user's own commitments and tradeoffs.

### The Three Big Decisions

Early life and career are dominated by a few choices: where to live, who to be
with, and what work to do. These choices deserve far more thinking time than
ordinary tasks because they shape the user's environment, relationships,
opportunities, and identity. [Almanack, pp. 74-75]

Decision questions:

- What city, network, or online environment compounds the user's learning?
- Which collaborators increase trust, ambition, taste, and integrity?
- Which work gives the user specific knowledge, leverage, accountability, and
  ownership?
- What must the user say no to so these decisions can receive enough attention?

## Work as Play

The strongest path is work that feels like play to the user and looks like work
to others. This is not laziness. It is the alignment of curiosity, energy,
identity, and market need. A person can sustain intensity for years when the
activity is intrinsically interesting. [Almanack, pp. 55-57, 76-80]

Advisor interpretation:

- Intrinsic motivation increases persistence and quality.
- Work done for its own sake often becomes the user's best work.
- Authenticity reduces direct competition because nobody else can be the user.
- Money can become a byproduct of useful play when leverage and market demand
  are attached. [Almanack, pp. 56, 76-80]

Traps:

- Confusing entertainment with enduring curiosity.
- Pursuing the hot market without the underlying obsession.
- Treating passion as sufficient without scale, ownership, accountability, or
  demand.
- Staying in a respectable path that is "just good enough" to keep the user from
  building their own thing. [Almanack, pp. 55-57, 75-80]

Prompt pattern:

```text
What can you do for years because it is interesting even before it pays? Now,
where does society need that ability, and how can it be scaled?
```

## Luck

The goal is to become wealthy in nearly every plausible universe, not only in
the one where blind luck hits. Part I describes four luck modes: accidental luck,
luck from motion, luck from prepared perception, and luck attracted by a unique
character or reputation. [Almanack, pp. 82-85]

Operating model:

- Blind luck cannot be managed.
- Motion creates collision surface area.
- Skill makes the user notice opportunities others miss.
- Character, brand, and reputation cause opportunities to seek the user out.
  [Almanack, pp. 82-85]

Advisor move: convert "I need luck" into a plan for surface area, skill, and
reputation.

Decision questions:

- What activity increases the number of useful collisions?
- What domain expertise would make the user sensitive to chances others miss?
- What public proof would make opportunity route through the user?
- What reputation would make strangers trust the user with a high-value deal?
- What high-integrity behavior must be repeated for years before it compounds?

## Patience

Patience is required after the user has assembled the right pieces. Great people
and good compounding systems often work, but not on the schedule the user wants.
Counting too closely drains patience. [Almanack, pp. 87-91]

The operating stance is "impatience with actions, patience with results." Act
when action is available; move quickly on inspiration; do the work with full
attention. Then allow markets, trust, product quality, and relationships time to
absorb the work. [Almanack, pp. 181-182]

Patience checklist:

- Is the user waiting because action is unclear, or because results take time?
- What action can be done today without needing permission from the market?
- What result depends on other people, adoption, trust, or complex systems?
- Is the user counting too closely and burning emotional fuel?
- Has the user created a system they can keep playing without needing immediate
  validation?

## Risk and Ruin

Take rationally optimistic bets with large upside, but avoid ruin. Do not risk
jail, health, catastrophic capital loss, or permanent reputation damage for a
single opportunity. The wealth game requires the user to stay in the game long
enough for compounding to work. [Almanack, pp. 66-67]

Advisor move: separate ordinary failure from ruin. Encourage accountability and
visible risk where the downside is survivable; reject bets that can remove the
user's ability to keep playing.

Risk questions:

- What is the upside if this works?
- What is the worst credible downside?
- Can the user recover from that downside?
- Does this risk reputation, health, legal standing, or all capital?
- Is the risk needed for accountability, or is it just gambling?

## Operating Checklist

Use this checklist to audit a user's career, company, project, or offer:

- **Value:** What does society want that it cannot yet get? [Almanack, pp.
  31-39]
- **Authenticity:** Why is the user unusually suited to provide it? [Almanack,
  pp. 40-45]
- **Learning:** What foundation or edge must be built? [Almanack, pp. 44-45]
- **Market:** Why now? What changed in technology, culture, distribution, or
  demand? [Almanack, pp. 38-39, 55-57]
- **Build/Sell:** Who builds, who sells, and can the user do either? [Almanack,
  pp. 32, 63-64]
- **Accountability:** Where is the user's name, promise, or track record on the
  line? [Almanack, pp. 50-52]
- **Ownership:** What equity, IP, royalties, options, or business ownership does
  the user capture? [Almanack, pp. 53-54]
- **Leverage:** What multiplies output beyond hours? [Almanack, pp. 55-67]
- **Judgment:** Which few decisions dominate the outcome? [Almanack, pp. 67-69,
  93-99]
- **Focus:** What must be ignored, delegated, automated, or declined? [Almanack,
  pp. 69-75]
- **Long game:** Who and what can compound for a decade? [Almanack, pp. 46-49,
  84-87]
- **Luck surface:** How does the user create, spot, or attract opportunity?
  [Almanack, pp. 82-85]
- **Patience:** Can the user keep playing without counting too closely?
  [Almanack, pp. 87-91, 181-182]
- **Ruin:** What downside must be avoided absolutely? [Almanack, pp. 66-67]

## Common Traps

- Grinding before knowing what to work on. [Almanack, p. 30]
- Renting time and expecting financial freedom. [Almanack, pp. 31, 53-54]
- Seeking credentials for a field that rewards frontier learning. [Almanack,
  pp. 44-45]
- Competing by copying instead of escaping through authenticity. [Almanack, pp.
  43-44, 77-78]
- Mistaking labor leverage and headcount for progress. [Almanack, pp. 34, 58]
- Confusing capital raised with value created. [Almanack, pp. 58, 66-69]
- Taking meetings, errands, and favors that silently consume the user's future.
  [Almanack, pp. 36, 69-72]
- Networking before making something interesting. [Almanack, pp. 85-86]
- Playing status games around wealth creation. [Almanack, pp. 72-74]
- Counting favors, progress, or time until patience runs out. [Almanack, pp.
  75, 87-89]
- Loving money itself instead of using money to buy freedom. [Almanack, pp.
  77-81, 89-91]

## Stories and Patterns

### Lost Fortunes, Stacked Chips

Naval describes early setbacks: one small fortune lost in the stock market and
another lost through partner conflict. His eventual wealth came from repeated
businesses, investments, options, and opportunities stacking over time, not one
single jackpot. [Almanack, pp. 69-70]

Advisor use: when the user wants one decisive win, redirect to repeatable wealth
creation and compounding opportunity flow.

### Elad Gil and Relationship Compounding

The example of Elad Gil shows a long-term relationship where each party rounds
in the other's favor. That generosity compounds into more deals, trust, and
opportunity. [Almanack, pp. 47-48]

Advisor use: ask whether the user is negotiating in a way that makes the next
ten interactions easier.

### Public Accountability in Philosophy and Crypto

Naval's shift from only discussing startups and investing to discussing broader
philosophy, psychology, and crypto involved public risk under his own name. The
same public exposure that risks criticism also creates upside when judgment is
right. [Almanack, pp. 50-52]

Advisor use: ask what idea the user is avoiding because it would attach their
name to a real position.

### The Real Estate Ladder

Part I's real estate ladder moves from hourly laborer to contractor to developer
to fund manager to technology-enabled platform. Each level adds more specific
knowledge, accountability, leverage, and ownership, creating a path from wages
to scalable upside. [Almanack, pp. 64-66]

Advisor use: map the user's industry into similar levels. Identify the next
level of leverage and ownership, not merely the next job title.

### The Deep-Sea Diver

The luck story uses a rare diver whose reputation causes a treasure opportunity
to route through them. The other person may have found the treasure by chance;
the diver's "luck" was created by rare skill and public reputation. [Almanack,
pp. 83-85]

Advisor use: ask what rare capability would make other people's luck become the
user's opportunity.

## Advisor Moves

- Ask "wealth, money, or status?" before solving a financial question.
- Translate hourly income into ownership, asset, or leverage terms.
- Ask what society wants and does not yet know how to get.
- Ask where the user can become the best by redefining the category narrowly.
- Separate hard work from correct direction.
- Audit whether the user is building, selling, or supporting someone else's
  asset.
- Identify the permissioned and permissionless leverage available.
- Ask which decision is worth a year of thought because it compounds for a
  decade.
- Push the user to protect empty calendar space for judgment.
- Convert networking advice into "make something interesting and show the
  work."
- Ask what reputation would make opportunity come inbound.
- Separate ordinary failure from risk of ruin.
- Use "impatience with actions, patience with results" to break waiting loops.

## Prompt Patterns

### Wealth Versus Money

```text
Are we solving for wealth, money, or status? Name the asset, the cash flow, and
the social-ranking motive separately.
```

### Leverage Audit

```text
For this plan, list the leverage from labor, capital, code, media, brand, and
distribution. Which one compounds without adding matching hours?
```

### Ownership Audit

```text
Where is the upside captured? Wages, equity, IP, royalties, options, carry,
revenue share, or business ownership? What happens if this works 100x better
than expected?
```

### Long-Term Game Test

```text
If you repeated this relationship, market, or project for ten years, would trust,
knowledge, reputation, and distribution compound? Or would you be starting over
each time?
```

### Productize Yourself

```text
What feels like play to you but looks like work to others? What does society
want from that? How can it become a product, asset, or public proof under your
name?
```

### Judgment Before Effort

```text
Before asking how hard to work, ask: are we pointed in the right direction? What
one decision would make the next year easier or irrelevant?
```

### Luck Surface Area

```text
What can you do this month to increase motion, improve opportunity recognition,
or build the reputation that makes the right opportunities come to you?
```

### Patience Split

```text
What action is available immediately, and what result must be allowed to
compound? Be impatient with the action and patient with the result.
```
