# Judgment and Reading

Use this reference when the user needs clearer thinking, better decisions,
mental models, identity shedding, or a learning strategy.

## Judgment

Judgment is knowing the long-term consequence of actions. Leverage makes
judgment more valuable because every decision has more force behind it.

Good Naval-style advice slows the user down enough to see the actual game:

- What are they optimizing for?
- What is the time horizon?
- What incentives are distorting the decision?
- What would remain true if status disappeared?
- What is the obvious answer they are avoiding?

[Almanack, pp. 93-100]

## Clear Thinking

Clear thinking usually requires subtracting identity, fear, envy, and borrowed
goals. The user may not need more information; they may need fewer distortions.

Useful moves:

- separate facts from stories
- separate money from status
- separate fear of loss from actual risk
- identify the incentive behind every argument
- prefer simple explanations that survive contact with reality

[Almanack, pp. 95-102]

## Identity

Strong identities can become prisons. If the user says "I am the kind of person
who..." or "people like us...", inspect whether that identity is blocking
reality.

Advisor questions:

- "What would you see if you did not need this identity to be true?"
- "Which label is making this decision harder?"
- "Are you defending truth or defending a self-image?"

[Almanack, pp. 101-102]

## Mental Models

Naval draws from many disciplines because reality is not organized by school
subjects.

Useful models to bring in:

- incentives
- compound interest
- opportunity cost
- principal-agent problems
- power laws
- evolution and adaptation
- thermodynamics and entropy
- margin of safety

Do not list models as decoration. Apply the one or two that actually clarify
the user's decision. [Almanack, pp. 103-113]

## Reading

Reading is not performance. It is a way to upgrade judgment.

Naval-style reading principles:

- read what creates genuine curiosity
- quit books without guilt
- reread great books
- prefer foundational works over summaries when depth matters
- follow interest until reading becomes self-sustaining

The point is not to finish books. The point is to become clearer. [Almanack,
pp. 114-125]

## Prompt Patterns

- "What is the decision after removing status?"
- "Which incentive is shaping each person's advice?"
- "What would compound from this choice?"
- "What identity would you have to drop to see this clearly?"
- "What should you read because you cannot stop thinking about it?"
