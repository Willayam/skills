# Clayton Christensen

## Who he was

Clayton Magleby Christensen was born on 6 April 1952 in Salt Lake City and died on 23 January 2020 in Boston. He studied economics at Brigham Young University, spent two years as a missionary in South Korea, read applied econometrics at Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar, and took an MBA at Harvard Business School in 1979. He worked at Boston Consulting Group, co-founded a materials science company, then returned to Harvard for a doctorate in 1992 and stayed for the rest of his career. He became the Kim B. Clark Professor of Business Administration in 2001 and co-founded the consultancy Innosight, the investment firm Rose Park Advisors, and the Christensen Institute.

His doctoral research on disk drives became The Innovator's Dilemma in 1997. It asked why well-run companies fail when a cheap, inferior technology appears, and answered that their good management is the cause. He spent twenty years extending that into a theory of growth, in The Innovator's Solution with Michael Raynor in 2003, a theory of customer choice, in Competing Against Luck in 2016, and a theory of why capital stops funding new markets, in the 2014 HBR article The Capitalist's Dilemma with Derek van Bever. A talk he gave to an HBS class in 2010 became How Will You Measure Your Life? in 2012. He was a devout Latter-day Saint, a father of five, soft-spoken, and insistent that management is a discipline built on causal theory rather than on data about the past.

## Core frameworks

### Disruption theory

What it says. Disruption is a process, not a product. His 2015 restatement in HBR reads: "'Disruption' describes a process whereby a smaller company with fewer resources is able to successfully challenge established incumbent businesses." The incumbent improves its product for its most demanding and most profitable customers and overshoots what many customers can use. An entrant takes root either at the low end, serving overshot customers with a good-enough product at a lower price, or in a new market, turning non-consumers into consumers. The incumbent, chasing margin upmarket, does not respond. The entrant improves along its own trajectory until mainstream customers adopt it. Only then has disruption occurred.

Sustaining innovation makes a good product better on the dimensions existing customers already pay for. The fifth blade in a razor. Incumbents almost always win sustaining battles. Disruptive innovation is initially worse on those dimensions and better on a different one, usually price, simplicity, convenience, or access. Incumbents almost always lose these, and not because they are asleep.

Why good management causes the dilemma. The resource allocation process is built to fund what the best customers want at the highest margin. A disruptive product offers lower margins, sells into markets too small to matter, and is rejected by the best customers. Every rational filter says no. From the 1997 book: "The reason is that good management itself was the root cause. Managers played the game the way it was supposed to be played."

The performance-oversupply point. Technology improves faster than customers can absorb. Where the product exceeds what customers can use, the basis of competition changes. Customers stop paying for performance and start paying for reliability, then convenience, then price. He wrote that "differentiation loses its meaning when the features and functionality have exceeded what the market demands." When your customers cannot use your improvements, your margin on those improvements is about to fall.

Why incumbents flee upmarket. Upper tiers have higher margins, larger customers, and a cost structure that already fits. When an entrant takes the bottom tier, ceding it and moving up is the rational move. Each retreat looks right. The sum of the retreats is death. He called it the asymmetry of motivation. Entrants are motivated to attack. Incumbents are motivated to flee.

How to run it. Is the product overshooting a material number of customers? Who are the non-consumers and what keeps them out? Is there an entrant worse on our axis and better on another, with a trajectory steep enough to reach our mainstream? If yes, does our allocation process fund a response or reject one? If it rejects, the response needs a separate organization.

### Resources, processes, and values

What it says. "Three classes of factors affect what an organization can and cannot do: its resources, its processes, and its values." Resources are what you can buy, hire, or move. Processes are the learned patterns of coordination and decision making. Values are the criteria by which people prioritize, above all what margin is acceptable and what opportunity is big enough to matter. Resources are flexible. Processes and values exist to make the same decision the same way every time, which is exactly what makes them unable to make a different one.

Why capabilities become disabilities. A process tuned to launch a sustaining product to a known customer cannot, by construction, launch a disruptive product into an unknown market. A value that says nothing under forty percent gross margin will kill every disruptive idea before it reaches the CEO, and nobody will have done anything wrong.

When to use which structure. Do the existing processes fit the new task? Do the existing values fit it? If both fit, use a lightweight team inside the mainstream organization. If processes do not fit but values do, form a heavyweight team, a dedicated group pulled out of their functions, colocated, and free to invent new processes while still judged by the parent's priorities. If values do not fit, because the opportunity is too small or too low-margin to compete for resources inside the parent, build an autonomous unit with its own cost structure, customers, and definition of success. From the 1997 book: "With few exceptions, the only instances in which mainstream firms have successfully established a timely position in a disruptive technology were those in which the firms' managers set up an autonomous organization charged with building a new and independent business around the disruptive technology."

Acquisitions follow the same logic. If you are buying resources, integrate them. If you are buying processes and values, keep the target separate, because integration will overwrite what you paid for.

### Jobs to be done

What it says. "When we buy a product, we essentially 'hire' something to get a job done." A job is the progress a person is trying to make in a specific circumstance. "Customers don't buy products or services; they pull them into their lives to make progress." The circumstance matters more than the customer's attributes. Every job has three dimensions. Functional, what must get done. Emotional, how the person wants to feel. Social, how the person wants to be seen. Most companies compete only on the functional dimension, which is why competitors converge on the same feature list.

The milkshake story. A fast-food chain wanted to sell more milkshakes. It surveyed buyers, made the shakes thicker and more chocolatey, and sales did not move. Bob Moesta, Christensen's collaborator, stood in a restaurant and watched. Nearly half the shakes sold before 8.30 in the morning, to people alone, who bought nothing else and drove off. The job was a long dull commute, a hand that needed something to do, and a stomach that needed to last until lunch. The shake took twenty minutes through a thin straw and did not crumble like a bagel or stick like a doughnut. The competition was bananas, bagels, and boredom. The afternoon shake had a different job, a parent saying yes to a child, and needed to be smaller and thinner. One product, two jobs, two sets of competitors, two designs.

Competing with non-consumption. The easiest customer to win hires nothing today, because anything beats nothing. Non-consumers lack the money, skill, time, or access to use the existing product. They do not show up in share data because they are not in the market. A product far worse than the incumbent's on the incumbent's dimensions, but that lets non-consumers do the job at all, grows where the incumbent cannot see.

How to find a job. Competing Against Luck names five places to look. Jobs in your own life. Non-consumption. Workarounds, where people have duct-taped their own solution. Negative jobs, things people want to not have to do. Unusual uses, where customers hire a product for something it was not built for. Then interview for the story, not the opinion. Walk backwards from the purchase to the first thought. "Watching how customers actually use a product provides much more reliable information than can be gleaned from a verbal interview or a focus group."

Why jobs beat demographic segmentation. In Marketing Malpractice, 2005, with Scott Cook and Taddy Hall, he argued that segmenting by product category or customer type produces correlations, not causes. A forty-year-old man with a certain income buys a certain newspaper, and nothing about that fact says why. Companies that segment by job find their market much larger than they thought and their share much smaller. His later warning applies to every dashboard: "Data has an annoying way of conforming itself to support whatever point of view we want it to support."

### Emergent versus deliberate strategy

What it says. Deliberate strategy is planned from analysis. Emergent strategy comes from the day-to-day choices of people who see what customers actually do. Where the market is known, deliberate works. In a new market the first strategy is almost always wrong, and the winning one emerges from contact with customers. The Innovator's Solution found that most successful companies won with a strategy different from the one they started with. The danger is not a wrong first strategy. Everyone has one. The danger is spending so much on it that nothing is left when the right one appears.

Discovery-driven planning. The tool came from Rita McGrath and Ian MacMillan. Conventional planning projects financials from assumptions. Discovery-driven planning reverses it. Start from the result you require. List every assumption that has to prove true. Rank by how lethal and how cheap to test. Test the cheap lethal ones first, before you spend. From the 1997 book: "They must be plans for learning rather than plans for implementation."

Patient for growth, impatient for profit. Good money for a new venture is patient for growth and impatient for profit. Impatience for profit forces the team to find customers who pay now, which keeps fixed costs low and tests assumptions early. Patience for growth gives the emergent strategy time to appear. Bad money is the reverse. The corporate parent needs the venture big soon and has cash to burn, so it scales the wrong strategy, loses heavily, and cancels. His rule is to launch new growth businesses while the core is still healthy, because only then can a company afford patience.

### Integration, modularity, and where the money goes

What it says. When a product is not yet good enough, proprietary and interdependent architectures win, because making the parts work together is the constraint. When the product is more than good enough, customers pay for speed, customization, and price instead. The architecture goes modular, interfaces standardize, and assembly commoditizes.

The law of conservation of attractive profits. When modularity and commoditization cause attractive profits to disappear at one stage in the value chain, the opportunity to earn attractive profits with proprietary products usually emerges at an adjacent stage. The money moves to wherever the next not-good-enough problem lives. When PC assembly commoditized, profit moved to the microprocessor and the operating system.

How to run it. Ask where your product is still not good enough. That is where integration pays and where to hold proprietary control. Ask where it has overshot. That is where you will be modularized, and where to stop investing in differentiation. Then look one step up and one step down the chain for the newly not-good-enough part. That is where the money is migrating. He titled a chapter of the 2003 book with the Gretzky line, skate to where the money will be.

### The Capitalist's Dilemma

What it says. The 2014 article sorts innovation by what it does to capital and jobs. Performance-improving innovations replace old products with better ones and create few jobs. Efficiency innovations sell mature products to the same customers at lower cost. They eliminate jobs and free capital. Market-creating innovations make a complicated, expensive product simple and affordable enough that a new class of people can buy it. They create new customers, new jobs, and new demand for the capital that efficiency frees.

Why efficiency chases efficiency. Freed capital should flow into market creation. It does not, because the ratios used to judge investments, return on net assets, return on invested capital, internal rate of return, all reward using less capital and paying back sooner. Efficiency pays back in two years and shrinks the asset base. Market creation takes five to ten years and needs capital up front. So the metrics rank efficiency higher every time, the freed capital goes back into more efficiency, and the company hollows out while the spreadsheets improve. His line: "Doing the right thing for long-term prosperity is the wrong thing for most investors, according to the tools used to guide investments." The tools assume capital is scarce. It is abundant and cheap, and a company that measures itself by how little it uses will optimize itself out of growth.

Why large optimized companies stop growing. Each efficiency innovation was correct and raised the ratios. Together they removed the slack, the tolerance for small markets, and the patience that market creation needs. The company now has more free cash than ever and nowhere its metrics will let it go except buybacks and more efficiency. That is not a failure of will. It is a measurement system doing what it was built to do.

### The growth imperative and why core engines stall

What it says. The Innovator's Solution opens with the finding that expected growth is already priced into a stock, so managers must beat expectations just to hold the price, and that only about one company in ten sustains above-average growth over a decade. When the core stalls, the pressure to find new growth arrives at the moment the company can least afford patience. Ventures launched then are held to the core's margin and scale expectations, which kills them. "Once a company's growth has stopped, the game as we have known it is over."

Why the engine stalls. The core overshoots its customers, so sustaining improvements earn less each year. The values ratchet upward, so fewer opportunities qualify as big enough. The allocation process, built to feed the core, starves anything that does not resemble it. The cure is not a bigger bet. It is a portfolio of small bets launched while the core is strong, judged by their own metrics, and given time.

Skate to where the money will be. Ask where the product will overshoot next, which stage will commoditize, which adjacent stage becomes the new constraint, and where the non-consumers are whose entry creates a new value network. Build the cost structure and business model for that future, not this present.

## Teaching stories

Steel minimills. Integrated mills made every grade of steel in vast plants. Minimills melted scrap in electric arc furnaces at roughly twenty percent lower cost, but their steel was poor. The only market that would take it was rebar, which nobody inspects. The integrated mills ceded rebar and their margins rose. Then the minimills improved into angle iron, structural beams, and finally sheet steel. At each step the integrated mills retreated upmarket and their margins improved for a while. Nucor took more than forty years to match the largest integrated mills in revenue, then passed them. Every retreat was rational.

Disk drives. His doctoral study covered every disk drive company from 1976 into the 1990s. The 14-inch makers served mainframes and ignored the 8-inch drive, which had less capacity and a smaller size mainframes did not need, so entrants sold it to minicomputer makers. The 8-inch makers then ignored the 5.25-inch drive, which Seagate sold to PC makers, and so on to 3.5-inch drives for laptops. In every generation the incumbents had prototypes and could not get funding, because their best customers did not want them. In every generation the smaller drive improved faster than the market's need and overtook it.

Honda Supercub. In 1959 Honda sent a team to Los Angeles to sell large motorcycles against Harley-Davidson. The bikes leaked oil and burned clutches on American highways. The team had brought 50cc Supercubs for errands and rode them in the dirt hills to blow off steam. Neighbors asked where to buy one. A Sears buyer called. Honda began selling Supercubs through sporting goods stores to people who had never owned a motorcycle. The deliberate strategy failed. The emergent one built a market of non-consumers and let Honda move upmarket later.

Sony's transistor radio. In 1955 Sony launched a battery-powered pocket transistor radio with tinny sound. RCA and the other tube-radio makers had transistors too and used them in laboratories to try to make them good enough for the living room. Sony sold to teenagers, who could not afford a tabletop radio and preferred poor sound out of their parents' earshot. Non-consumers. By the time transistors were good enough for the living room, the tube-radio makers were gone.

Toyota. Toyota entered the United States in the 1960s with the Corona, a small cheap car Detroit ignored while building larger, more profitable ones. Toyota moved up through the Corolla, Camry, and Avalon to the Lexus, which competed for the most profitable segment. The incumbents fled upmarket at each step, then ran out of upmarket. When Toyota became the incumbent, Hyundai and Kia entered beneath it on the same path.

Intel Celeron. In 1997 Christensen presented the minimill story to Intel's senior managers. Andy Grove said in effect, I get it, and here is what it means for Intel. Sub-thousand-dollar PCs were appearing with chips from Cyrix and AMD, and Intel's best customers did not want a cheap chip. Grove made Rebar the internal codename for a low-end processor, the Celeron, launched in 1998 in a separate unit so the mainstream sales force could not strangle it. The point is not that theory predicted Intel's move. It is that a manager with the theory could see what to do before the data told him.

Southern New Hampshire University. Paul LeBlanc, a friend of Christensen who later put him on the board, became president in 2003 of a small college with a tiny online program. The traditional student hires college for a coming-of-age experience. The online student, thirty-something, working, often a parent or a veteran, hires it to finish a credential without wrecking the rest of life. Same product, different job. SNHU separated the online business from the campus, gave it its own processes and values, and built for the second job, down to answering an enquiry within minutes. By 2016 revenue approached four hundred and fifty million dollars and most students were people who otherwise would not have gone.

## His voice

He spoke slowly and asked more questions than he answered. He did not tell an executive the answer. He walked them through a causal mechanism and let them find their own situation inside it. Nearly every sentence had a "because" in it. He distrusted data not because it lies but because it only describes the past. He treated business as a science with experiments, anomalies, and theories that improve when they are wrong, and he treated every anomaly as a gift. He was warm, self-deprecating, devout, and unafraid to say that most of what passes for strategy is a correlation dressed as a cause.

## Questions he would ask about growth in a large, optimized consumer platform

He would not start with the growth target. He would start with the mechanism that produced the current business and ask whether it can produce a different one.

- Of your last twenty investments, how many were sustaining, efficiency, or market-creating? What does the ratio say about what your allocation process is built to approve?
- Who are your non-consumers? Not the people who churned. The people who never came. What keeps them out, money, skill, time, or access?
- On which dimension have you overshot your mainstream users? Where do they no longer notice or pay for improvements?
- What job do your heaviest users hire you for, and what job do your lightest users hire you for? Are those the same job?
- What are people hiring instead of you, including nothing? What would you have to be worse at to serve them?
- If a unit brought your capital committee a forty million dollar opportunity at half your gross margin, what would happen to it? Answer from the values, not the slide.
- Which metric would have to go down for a market-creating business to be funded, and who owns that metric?
- Where in your value chain is the product still not good enough, and where has it commoditized? Where is the profit going next, and are you positioned there?
- What has the platform let others do that you never let yourself do because it looked too small?
- What is the first strategy you would launch with, and which of its assumptions is cheapest to test and most lethal if false?
- What would you have to stop doing? Not add. Stop.

## How he would think about AI and agentic coding

He would refuse to call it disruptive until someone showed him the foothold. The question is not whether AI is good, but what it changes in the cost structure of building, and what the theories predict follows.

A collapse in the cost of building is an enabling technology, like the transistor or online learning. It does not decide whether an incumbent or an entrant wins. It sets the steepness of whichever trajectory it enables. The 2015 article noted that continuous casting improved slowly, so Nucor took forty years, while PC technology improved fast, so Compaq caught DEC in twelve. Agentic coding improves very fast. Whatever it enables will move from foothold to mainstream in years, not decades, and an incumbent will have far less time to notice than the theory usually grants.

On modularity. When software is expensive to write, integrated systems win, because making the parts work together is the constraint. When software becomes cheap and fast to write, the constraint moves. Building stops being the hard part. Knowing which job to build for, holding the customer relationship, owning proprietary data, and owning distribution become the hard parts. The law of conservation of attractive profits says the money leaves the layer that commoditized, now application code, and reappears in the adjacent layers that are still not good enough. For a consumer platform, its own software is no longer its advantage. Its brand, its data about jobs, and its place in the customer's life are, and those must be defended and monetized differently.

On non-consumption. Cheap building makes it economic to serve jobs that were too small or too specific to serve when engineers cost three hundred thousand dollars a year. A large platform will look at those markets and see, correctly, that each is too small to matter. That is the dilemma. Entrants with agentic tooling will serve them anyway, at a cost structure the platform cannot match, and will grow under the platform's own price umbrella. The response the theory gives is the usual one. A unit with a different cost structure and different values, using the platform's resources but not its processes, and allowed to be small.

On which jobs become addressable. The emotional and social dimensions of jobs were underserved because serving them was expensive. A product that adapts to one person's circumstance, in language, in real time, used to be a research project. Now it is a weekend. The jobs that become addressable are the ones defined by circumstance rather than segment, and a company holding rich data about circumstances has a head start, if its values let it use that data for small jobs. He would ask the platform whether it has ever built for a job hired by a hundred thousand people, and if not, whether it now can.

On the incumbent's dilemma. The cost of building was never the incumbent's constraint. Its allocation process and values were, and still are. Agentic coding makes engineers cheaper for everyone, which lowers the barrier beneath the incumbent and raises the speed at which entrants climb. It does not change what the capital committee will approve. So the prediction is that a well-run consumer platform will use AI first for efficiency, cutting cost per feature, which raises the ratios and frees capital. It will spend that capital on more efficiency and on sustaining improvements to the core. Some years in, it will discover that small entrants used the same tools to serve the non-consumers and low-end jobs it ignored, and are moving upmarket. The way out is to sort every AI investment into one of the three kinds and protect a market-creating portfolio from the efficiency logic that will otherwise absorb all of it. The theory does not say what to do. It says what will happen if you do nothing different, and why.
