# Worldly Wisdom And Inversion

Use this reference for mental models, inversion, circle of competence,
multidisciplinary reasoning, and learning habits.

## Latticework Of Models

Munger's core educational complaint was that people memorize isolated facts
without a structure that makes them usable. His remedy is a latticework:
experience must hang on models from many disciplines, not on slogans or single
theories. [Worldly Wisdom 1994]

The practical rule:

- Do not ask, "What is my opinion?"
- Ask, "Which models explain this, and which models would falsify my preferred
  explanation?"

Important model families:

- **Mathematics:** compounding, probability, permutations, expected value,
  decision trees, base rates.
- **Psychology:** incentives, social proof, denial, commitment, envy,
  availability, vividness, stress, authority.
- **Economics:** opportunity cost, supply and demand, scale, competition,
  incentives, agency costs.
- **Accounting:** cash, depreciation, owner earnings, leverage, asset intensity,
  fraud risk.
- **Engineering:** margin of safety, redundancy, bottlenecks, feedback loops,
  failure modes.
- **Biology:** evolution, niches, adaptation, ecosystems, predator/prey
  dynamics.
- **History:** cycles, incentives under stress, institutional decay, war,
  bubbles, technological transitions.
- **Law and ethics:** agency, fiduciary duty, trust, contracts, reputation,
  incentives created by rules.

## Avoid The One-Model Mind

Munger repeatedly warned that one model becomes a hammer. The user should be
pressed to name at least three models that point in the same direction before
treating an answer as robust. [Worldly Wisdom 1994]

If only one model supports the user's decision:

- Identify the model.
- Ask what reality it might be torturing to fit.
- Add disconfirming models from psychology, economics, and engineering.

## Inversion

Inversion is the default Munger move.

Instead of:

- "How do I make this succeed?"

Ask:

- "How would I make this fail?"
- "Where is the ruin?"
- "What would make me regret this?"
- "Which incentives would guarantee bad behavior?"
- "What obvious stupidity can I remove now?"

Then answer by avoidance, structure, and margin of safety.

Common inversion maps:

- **Career:** To guarantee mediocrity, chase status, ignore feedback, stop
  reading, work with low-integrity people, and switch games whenever effort is
  required.
- **Startup:** To guarantee failure, build for yourself, ignore distribution,
  hire for credentials over ownership, hide bad metrics, and raise costs before
  evidence.
- **Relationship:** To guarantee misery, keep score, reward resentment, avoid
  hard conversations, and choose partners with incompatible incentives.
- **Investing:** To guarantee bad results, use leverage, buy what you do not
  understand, follow crowds, ignore fees and taxes, and sell quality because of
  boredom.
- **Health:** To guarantee decline, sleep poorly, eat carelessly, avoid exercise,
  rationalize addiction, and seek heroic interventions after neglect.

## Circle Of Competence

The size of the circle matters less than knowing its boundary. A small,
well-marked circle beats a large imaginary one.
[Berkshire 1996 Letter]

When the user is outside the circle:

- Reduce bet size.
- Seek a trustworthy expert who has no incentive to mislead.
- Use checklists and base rates.
- Demand more margin of safety.
- Avoid irreversible commitments.

Expanding the circle requires repeated exposure, feedback, and reading across
disciplines. Confidence without feedback is not competence.

## Reading And Compounding Knowledge

Munger's model of wisdom is compounding. Reading is not decorative; it is how a
person acquires vicarious experience from dead people, distant industries, and
past mistakes.

Use this as advice:

- Read broadly enough that no single profession owns your mind.
- Prefer durable books and primary sources over commentary.
- Keep a running inventory of your mistakes.
- Revisit core ideas until they become automatic under stress.
- Treat multidisciplinary reading as model acquisition, not quote collection.
- Use books and primary sources to expand the circle slowly, then test the
  models against reality.

Munger's reading examples ranged outside finance, including history, science,
psychology, and anthropology. That matters: the skill should not solve every
problem as if it were an investing puzzle. [CNBC Reading]

## Planck Knowledge Versus Chauffeur Knowledge

Munger used the Planck/chauffeur story to distinguish real understanding from
fluent imitation. A person may repeat the right words and still lack the model
needed to act under novel conditions. [USC 2007]

Use this when the user has copied language from experts, consultants, books, or
competitors:

- Ask whether they can explain the causal mechanism.
- Ask what would falsify the claim.
- Ask what changes when the context changes.
- Treat fluent explanation without prediction as chauffeur knowledge.

## Decision Use

When applying this reference:

1. Name the user's implied model.
2. Add models from at least three disciplines.
3. Invert the desired outcome.
4. Mark the circle-of-competence boundary.
5. Recommend the action that removes the most stupidity with the least
   irreversible commitment.
