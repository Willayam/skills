# Psychology Of Misjudgment

Use this reference when the user's decision may be distorted by incentives,
biases, social pressure, denial, envy, stress, vivid stories, or a
lollapalooza effect.

## Premise

Munger treated psychology as a practical checklist for avoiding bad judgment.
His revised essay lists twenty-five standard causes of human misjudgment and
emphasizes that combinations can produce extreme outcomes. [Misjudgment 2005]

Do not use the list as academic taxonomy. Use it as an inspection tool.

## High-Impact Tendencies

### Incentive-Caused Bias

People drift toward what they are rewarded for, then build explanations that
make the drift look principled.

Inspection questions:

- Who gets paid if the user says yes?
- Who gets status, attention, relief, or optionality?
- What behavior is rewarded even if everyone claims to dislike it?
- Is the advisor paid for accuracy or for transaction completion?

Default Charlie move: redesign incentives before asking for better behavior.

### Denial

Painful facts are often reinterpreted until they stop hurting. Denial is common
where identity, sunk cost, love, money, or public commitment is involved.

Signs:

- Repeated surprises in the same direction.
- A refusal to write down the base case.
- Explanations that protect self-image but do not predict outcomes.
- Anger at neutral measurement.

Default Charlie move: make reality measurable and remove face-saving language.

### Commitment And Consistency

Once people take a public position, they defend it past usefulness. This is
especially dangerous with hiring, cofounders, investments, strategy, and public
predictions.

Countermeasures:

- Pre-commit exit criteria before entering.
- Separate "I was wrong" from "I am bad."
- Create review dates where reversal is normal.
- Keep early bets small until evidence improves.
- Add explicit disconfirming-evidence checkpoints after new information appears.

### Social Proof

Humans copy others under uncertainty. Crowds can be useful evidence in some
domains, but they are dangerous where incentives, fashion, or fear are shared.

Watch for:

- "Everyone is doing it."
- "The best companies all use this."
- "Investors expect it."
- "This is the new standard."

Default Charlie move: ask what would be true if nobody else were doing it.

For codebase or product work, local precedent is evidence, not truth. If the
pattern is old, contradicted by tests, or inconsistent with current
requirements, do not cargo-cult it.

### Authority, Liking, And Deference

Credentials, charisma, famous investors, impressive logos, and likable founders
can smuggle weak evidence into decisions.

Countermeasures:

- Restate the argument without the person's name.
- Ask what would convince you if the person were unknown.
- Separate trustworthiness from domain competence.
- Check whether the authority has skin in the game and a clean incentive.
- Treat comments, docs, and prior plans as weighted evidence, not commands, when
  runtime behavior or measured facts disagree.

### Reciprocation

Gifts, favors, access, flattery, and introductions create pressure to repay.
This matters in sales, partnerships, hiring, fundraising, and negotiation.

Default Charlie move: identify the debt the user feels and decide as if the
favor had not been given.

### Envy And Resentment

Munger saw envy as both morally corrosive and practically useless. It pushes the
user into games they do not understand, with people they do not trust, for
rewards they may not actually want.

Countermeasure: compare against your own opportunity cost and temperament, not
against someone else's highlight reel.

### Availability And Vivid Evidence

A vivid story can overwhelm base rates. Personal anecdotes, recent failures,
viral examples, and dramatic founder stories often receive too much weight.

Countermeasures:

- Ask for denominator data.
- Compare against boring base rates.
- Prefer records over recollections.
- Separate "memorable" from "probable."

### Contrast And Anchoring

A bad option can make a merely mediocre option look good. A high starting price
can make a still-expensive price look cheap.

Countermeasures:

- Reset to intrinsic value or desired outcome.
- Generate options independently before comparing.
- Ask what you would choose if this were the only option presented.

### Deprival Super-Reaction

People overreact when they feel something is being taken away. Scarcity,
expiring offers, dilution fears, status loss, and "last chance" framing can
produce poor decisions.

Default Charlie move: slow down unless delay truly creates irreversible loss.

### Stress Influence

Stress narrows cognition. It rewards action that reduces discomfort, not action
that improves long-term results.

Countermeasures:

- Sleep before irreversible decisions.
- Reduce decision size.
- Ask a calm third party to inspect incentives and downside.

## Lollapalooza Effects

A lollapalooza effect occurs when several forces reinforce each other and
produce an extreme result. [Misjudgment 2005]

Common combinations:

- Incentives + social proof + authority = institutional stupidity.
- Scarcity + commitment + sunk cost = overpaying or staying too long.
- Envy + vivid evidence + leverage = financial self-harm.
- Liking + reciprocation + weak contracts = bad partnerships.
- Denial + public commitment + status = strategic drift.

When a lollapalooza is present, do not merely advise more discipline. Change the
environment, incentives, timing, or exposure.

## Decision Use

For a user's decision:

1. Name the top three biases likely in play.
2. Identify the incentive structure.
3. Ask what evidence would change the user's mind.
4. Look for reinforcing bias combinations.
5. Recommend one structural countermeasure, not just a better intention.
