# Storytelling, Keynotes, and Narrative

Use this reference when the user is launching a product, pitching an idea,
preparing a keynote or demo, writing marketing copy, naming something, or
trying to make an audience feel why a thing matters before explaining what it
is. Do not use this file as a license for theatrics. The Jobs pattern is
narrative discipline in service of substance, not stagecraft as a substitute
for it.

Source note: Paraphrased operating notes drawn from public Apple keynotes
([MacWorld 2007], [iPad 2010], [iMac 1998], [iPod 2001]), the [Think Different]
campaign launch and "Here's to the Crazy Ones" voiceover, the [1984 Macintosh]
Super Bowl ad, [WWDC 1997] fireside chat, [AllThingsD D5] and [AllThingsD D8]
interviews, [Stanford 2005] commencement, Walter Isaacson's biography
[Isaacson], Brent Schlender and Rick Tetzeli's `Becoming Steve Jobs`
[Schlender], and Carmine Gallo's `The Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs`
[Gallo]. Citations point to where the move comes from. The user should verify
verbatim quotation against the original recordings or texts before publishing.

## Core Principles

- Lead with why before what. Jobs framed Apple's purpose ("people with passion
  can change the world for the better") before product specs. The product is
  the proof of the why, not the headline. [Think Different] [WWDC 1997]
- Marketing is the expression of values, not a pitch. The 1997 "Think
  Different" relaunch positioned Apple's brand as honoring people who push the
  human race forward, before any new product was ready. [Think Different]
  [Isaacson]
- A story has a villain. The [1984 Macintosh] ad cast conformity and central
  control as the antagonist; the Mac was liberation. [MacWorld 2007] cast the
  smartphone status quo (plastic keyboards, terrible software) as the villain
  before revealing iPhone. [Gallo]
- Demos are truth. A working product on stage is a higher form of evidence than
  a promise. Jobs reserved time for live use, not just slides. [MacWorld 2007]
  [iPad 2010]
- Simplicity is a position, not a style. Few words on a slide, one idea per
  beat, one product per moment. [Gallo]
- Anticipation is structure. Build to a reveal; do not bury it. The audience
  should feel the climax coming and still be surprised by the form. [MacWorld
  2007] [Gallo]
- Naming is positioning. Names are short, evocative, ownable, and human (Mac,
  iMac, iPod, iPhone, iPad). The lowercase "i" tied a family of products to
  internet, individual, and identity in 1998. [iMac 1998] [Isaacson]
- Products people love beats market share as a goal. The 1997 fireside chat
  framed Apple's job as making products customers love, with market share as a
  consequence, not a target. [WWDC 1997]
- Rehearse until rehearsal disappears. Days of practice for every major
  keynote; every transition, every demo, every joke timed. The performance
  looks effortless because the work was done off-stage. [Gallo] [Schlender]
- Tell origin stories. The Stanford address used three personal stories
  (connecting dots, love and loss, death) to deliver a worldview without
  preaching. Story carries argument that bullet points cannot. [Stanford 2005]

## Keynote Anatomy

The Jobs keynote is a repeatable structure. Use it for launches, pitches,
all-hands, and investor decks.

1. **Set the headline.** One sentence the audience could repeat tomorrow.
   "Today, Apple is going to reinvent the phone." [MacWorld 2007]
2. **Name the villain.** State what is wrong with the current world: a problem,
   a frustration, a status quo. Make the audience feel it. [Gallo] [1984
   Macintosh]
3. **Promise the journey.** "I have a few things to share with you today."
   Signal scope without revealing the punchline. [MacWorld 2007]
4. **Build with a rule of three.** Three products, three features, three
   reasons. The iPhone reveal: "a widescreen iPod with touch controls, a
   revolutionary mobile phone, a breakthrough internet communications device."
   Three is enough to feel substantial and few enough to remember. [MacWorld
   2007] [Gallo]
5. **Stage the reveal.** Repeat the pattern until the audience anticipates the
   beat, then collapse it. "These are not three separate devices. This is one
   device. And we are calling it iPhone." [MacWorld 2007]
6. **Demonstrate, do not describe.** Pick up the product. Use it on stage. Let
   the artifact argue for itself. [iPad 2010] [iPod 2001]
7. **Provide the heroic frame.** Position the user, not the company, as the
   hero of the story. The product gives them new capability. [Think Different]
8. **Land the climax with "one more thing."** Hold one surprise back for the
   end. Use sparingly; overuse decays the spell. [Gallo]
9. **Close on the why.** Return to purpose. End on what this enables in human
   terms, not in spec terms. [Stanford 2005] [WWDC 1997]

## Voice and Signature Phrases

Operating phrases the user can adapt. Do not parrot. Adapt the move.

- "Today, [we / our company] [reinvents / introduces] [category]." Stake the
  claim in the first sentence. [MacWorld 2007]
- "I have a few things to share with you today." Open a frame; invite
  attention. [MacWorld 2007]
- "Every once in a while, a revolutionary product comes along that changes
  everything." Set the scale before the thing arrives. [MacWorld 2007]
- "Are you getting it? These are not three separate devices." The collapse
  move; reveals that what looked like a list is actually one thing. [MacWorld
  2007]
- "And one more thing." Reserved for late-keynote surprise; do not promise it
  in advance. [Gallo]
- "It just works." Plain language for the experience claim; only earned if the
  demo holds up. [iPad 2010]
- "Boom." Punctuation for a successful demo step. Confidence sounds like
  brevity, not adjectives. [Gallo]
- "It's the intersection of technology and the liberal arts." Position the
  product as humanist, not just technical. [iPad 2010]
- "A bicycle for the mind." Frame the computer as an amplifier of human
  capability, not a replacement for it. [Isaacson]
- "Here's to the crazy ones." Frame the audience and the brand as one tribe of
  people who change things. [Think Different]

## Stories

### iPhone Reveal, MacWorld 2007

Jobs walked the audience through "three revolutionary products" — a wide
screen iPod, a phone, an internet device — repeating the list until the
audience expected three icons, then revealed one device. The reveal worked
because the structure had been rehearsed into the audience's expectation
before being broken. Before the reveal he spent minutes on what was wrong
with current smartphones: bad keyboards, bad software, hard to use. The
villain was made vivid, then defeated. [MacWorld 2007] [Gallo]

Advisor use: when a product fuses categories, do not list the categories on a
slide. Stage them as separate promises and collapse them into one.

### Think Different, 1997

Apple was near insolvency. Rather than ship a new product first, Jobs
relaunched the brand around values: honoring "the crazy ones, the misfits, the
rebels" who push the world forward. The campaign reasserted who Apple was
before reasserting what Apple sold. [Think Different] [Isaacson] [Schlender]

Advisor use: when a company has lost the plot, brand work that names values
clearly can precede product work. Only do this if real product work is coming;
otherwise it reads as cope.

### 1984 Macintosh

Aired once nationally during the Super Bowl. The ad did not show the product.
It dramatized a world of conformity broken by an athlete with a hammer. The
Mac was the implied liberator. The ad established the company's posture:
underdog, humanist, against centralized control. [1984 Macintosh] [Isaacson]

Advisor use: launch ads can earn attention by dramatizing the world the
product enters, not by listing what the product does.

### iPod Reveal, 2001

"1,000 songs in your pocket." A single, concrete claim that compressed the
entire value proposition into one sentence a person could feel. Not "5 GB
hard drive." Not "MP3 player." A unit the listener could imagine. [iPod 2001]
[Gallo]

Advisor use: convert specs into human-scale outcomes. "1,000 songs" beats "5
GB." "All day battery" beats "10.4 hours." Translate the number into the
felt benefit.

### iMac, 1998

Translucent, colored, friendly, a handle on top. Jobs framed it as
re-introducing the computer to people who had been alienated by beige boxes.
The lowercase "i" stood for internet, individual, instruct, inform, inspire —
naming as positioning. [iMac 1998] [Isaacson]

Advisor use: industrial design and naming are part of the story. A product
that looks different from the category signals a different posture toward the
user.

### Stanford, 2005

Three stories: dropping in on calligraphy class (connecting dots looking
backward), being fired from Apple (love what you do), the cancer diagnosis
(death as the engine of life). No product, no slide. Just structure: three
stories, one frame ("you can only connect them looking backwards"), one close
("stay hungry, stay foolish"). [Stanford 2005]

Advisor use: the three-story structure travels. It works for keynotes,
investor pitches, all-hands, and personal essays.

## How To Apply

### When the user is launching a product

1. Write the headline sentence first. If it cannot be said in one line, the
   product is not yet positioned.
2. Identify the villain. What about the current world is wrong, frustrating,
   or stuck? Make the audience feel it before the reveal.
3. Pick three. Three features, three reasons, three benefits. Cut the fourth.
4. Plan the demo. The product must do something live, not in a video. If it
   cannot demo, the launch is too early.
5. Translate specs to human outcomes. "1,000 songs in your pocket" not "5 GB."
6. Rehearse end-to-end three times before the dress rehearsal. Time every
   transition.

### When the user is pitching

1. Open with why this matters now. Not the team, not the round, not the deck.
2. Name the status quo and what it costs the user.
3. Show the product, even crudely. A video clip of real use beats a roadmap.
4. Land on a single sentence the listener will repeat to a partner.

### When the user is naming

1. Short. One or two syllables.
2. Evocative. Should hint at meaning without explaining.
3. Ownable. Search the term; the brand should be able to dominate the result.
4. Human. Avoid acronyms, version numbers, and committee compromises.
5. Family-aware. If this is one of many, the name should sit in a system
   (iMac, iPod, iPhone, iPad).

### When the user is writing copy

1. Plain words. Replace adjectives with concrete nouns and verbs.
2. One idea per sentence. One sentence per beat. One beat per slide.
3. Show the felt benefit, not the spec. The reader should picture themselves
   using it.
4. Read it aloud. If it does not sound like a person talking, rewrite.

### When the user is preparing a keynote

1. Outline the structure: headline, villain, journey, three, reveal, demo,
   why.
2. Strip the slides. Few words. Big images. Never read the slide.
3. Rehearse with the actual hardware, software, and clicker. Time it.
4. Plan one surprise. Hold it for the end.
5. End on the human frame, not the spec frame.

## Failure Modes

- **Theatre without substance.** The structure works only when the product
  delivers. A villain-reveal-demo arc on a thin product reads as hype and
  burns trust. Substance first; narrative is amplification, not replacement.
  [Schlender]
- **Imitating phrases without earning them.** "One more thing" used by anyone
  who has not earned the climax sounds like cosplay. Use the move; do not
  borrow the words.
- **Stagecraft replacing rehearsal.** The Jobs keynote looked effortless
  because of obsessive practice. Skipping rehearsal and importing the
  vocabulary is the opposite of the method. [Gallo]
- **Hero-of-the-company framing.** The story should make the user the hero,
  not the founder. Founder-hero pitches age badly and read as ego.
  [Think Different]
- **Spec-led storytelling.** Leading with megapixels, gigahertz, or
  parameter counts loses the audience that does not already care. Translate
  to outcome.
- **Overusing "revolutionary."** Reserve the word for moments that earn it.
  Inflated language deflates fast.
- **Hiding the demo.** A launch with no live use of the product signals it is
  not ready. Either delay or shrink the claim.
- **Confusing minimalism with thinness.** Few words on a slide is not the same
  as few ideas behind the slide. The depth lives in the work, not the deck.
  [Gallo]

## Use With Care

- Do not encourage users to imitate Jobs personally. Extract the moves —
  structure, demo, naming, rehearsal, why-before-what — and adapt them to the
  user's voice and product.
- Do not recommend mystery and secrecy as defaults. Jobs's secrecy served
  specific reveals; in most contexts, building in public earns more trust than
  it costs.
- Verify all verbatim quotations against the original keynote recordings and
  texts before publishing. Paraphrased operating notes are not a substitute
  for the source.
