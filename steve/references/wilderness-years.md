# The Wilderness Years: NeXT, Pixar, and What Made Jobs 2.0

Use this reference when the user has been fired, demoted, sidelined, lost a
company, watched a successor take their seat, or feels exiled between acts. Use
it also when the user is in the middle of a long, expensive, and unsexy bet
where the original plan has clearly failed but quitting would mean losing the
asset that eventually pays off.

The arc covered here runs from Jobs's 1985 firing through the 1997 return as
iCEO, with NeXT and Pixar as the two parallel laboratories where Jobs learned
what his first run at Apple did not teach him.

Source note: Public materials only. Primary citations: Jobs's 2005 Stanford
Commencement [Stanford 2005]; Brent Schlender and Rick Tetzeli, *Becoming Steve
Jobs* [Schlender]; Walter Isaacson, *Steve Jobs* [Isaacson]; Ed Catmull,
*Creativity, Inc.* [Catmull]; *The Lost Interview* (1995) [Lost Interview];
*Triumph of the Nerds* (1996) [Nerds 1996]; AllThingsD D5/D8 conversations
[D5/D8]; *Playboy* interview (February 1985) [Playboy 1985]; Alan Deutschman,
*The Second Coming of Steve Jobs* [Deutschman]; *The Pixar Story* documentary
(2007) [Pixar Story]. Quotes are paraphrased from operating notes unless marked
verbatim; verify originals before quoting on the record.

## When To Load This File

- The user has just been fired, pushed out, or stripped of a title.
- The user lost a startup, got demoted, or watched a board choose someone else.
- The user is in a long bet (3-10 years) that has stopped looking like a
  comeback story and started looking like a slow defeat.
- The user is asking whether to walk away from a project that is technically
  excellent but commercially failing.
- The user is between acts and wants to know what to do with the gap.
- The user is romanticizing the firing, expecting a Stanford-style redemption,
  and needs the failure-mode reminder that not every wilderness becomes a 2.0.
- The user has talented creative people working under them and is unsure
  whether to direct them or get out of their way.

## Core Thesis

The wilderness years are the period that turned a brilliant, abrasive,
narrow-spectrum founder into a leader who could come back and run Apple to its
peak. The lessons did not arrive through a single insight; they were produced
by twelve years of being wrong in expensive, public, and instructive ways.

Two laboratories ran in parallel:

- **NeXT** was Jobs as auteur with no countervailing force. It tested every
  Apple-era reflex (vertical integration, premium pricing, perfectionism,
  beautiful objects) without the volume, market timing, or distribution that
  had carried the Mac. It largely failed, but produced NeXTSTEP, which became
  the technical basis for Mac OS X. [Schlender; Isaacson]
- **Pixar** was Jobs as patron and financier rather than creative dictator.
  Catmull and Lasseter ran the creative work; Jobs funded, protected, and
  negotiated. It succeeded enormously and taught him what letting talent lead
  actually looks like. [Catmull; Pixar Story]

The maturation did not come from NeXT alone or Pixar alone. It came from
running both at once and watching the auteur model lose money while the patron
model produced *Toy Story*. [Schlender]

Schlender's central thesis: the Steve Jobs who came back in 1997 was not the
Steve Jobs who was fired in 1985. The wilderness made him strategic,
selectively patient, more willing to trust deep collaborators, and clearer
about which of his instincts were product genius and which were ego dressed up
as taste. [Schlender]

## The 1985 Firing

### Context

Jobs at thirty was running the Macintosh division as a separate kingdom inside
Apple, in open conflict with the Apple II group that produced most of the
revenue. He had recruited John Sculley from Pepsi in 1983 with the famous
"sugar water" line. By spring 1985 the Mac was selling below plan, the Lisa
was a write-off, and the board sided with Sculley. Jobs was stripped of
operating responsibility, kept on as chairman in name, and left Apple in
September 1985. [Isaacson; Playboy 1985]

### What Jobs Said About It Later

The canonical framing is the 2005 Stanford Commencement. Verbatim phrases
worth knowing:

- "How can you get fired from a company you started?" [Stanford 2005]
- "What had been the focus of my entire adult life was gone, and it was
  devastating." [Stanford 2005]
- "I didn't see it then, but it turned out that getting fired from Apple was
  the best thing that could have ever happened to me. The heaviness of being
  successful was replaced by the lightness of being a beginner again, less
  sure about everything. It freed me to enter one of the most creative periods
  of my life." [Stanford 2005]

Operator's note: Jobs framed this in 2005, twenty years after the fact, after
the comeback. In 1985 he was furious, humiliated, and considering a sailing
trip to forget about it. [Isaacson; Schlender] The Stanford framing is
retrospective truth, not real-time wisdom. Do not let a user in month two of
their own firing imitate the 2005 voice. They are still in 1985.

### Diagnostic Lessons From The Firing

- Founder status does not protect against board math when revenue is
  underperforming. [Isaacson]
- A divisional kingdom inside a larger company is structurally fragile when
  the parent business carries the cash. [Isaacson]
- Hiring a "professional CEO" without aligning on who has final product
  authority is a setup for the conflict that follows. [Isaacson; Playboy 1985]
- Charisma plus contempt is a short-term operating model. The same intensity
  that recruits the Mac team alienates the rest of the company. [Schlender]

## NeXT: Auteur Without A Countervailing Force

### What Jobs Built

Founded late 1985 with a small group of Apple defectors. The product was a
high-end Unix workstation aimed at higher education and research. The hardware
was the magnesium NeXT cube, an industrial-design landmark. The software stack
(NeXTSTEP, Objective-C frameworks, Interface Builder) was years ahead of its
peers. [Isaacson; Nerds 1996]

### What Worked

- NeXTSTEP and the object-oriented frameworks were genuinely excellent and
  later became the foundation of Mac OS X, iOS, and the modern Apple software
  stack after the 1996 acquisition. [Schlender; Isaacson]
- The development environment created a generation of engineers who would
  later staff Apple's renaissance.
- Tim Berners-Lee built the first web browser and web server on a NeXT
  machine. The platform punched above its installed base. [public record]

### What Failed

- The cube was a beautiful object priced and configured for a market that did
  not exist at scale. Higher education could not absorb the volume; enterprise
  was buying Sun and DEC. [Isaacson]
- Vertical integration without volume meant the custom factory in Fremont sat
  underutilized. The same integration logic that worked at Apple (because the
  Apple II funded it) destroyed cash at NeXT. [Isaacson; Schlender]
- Perfectionism without timing: NeXT shipped late and at a price the market
  would not bear, while the Wintel ecosystem was getting cheaper every quarter.
  [Nerds 1996]
- Hardware was eventually killed in 1993; NeXT pivoted to software-only
  (NeXTSTEP and later WebObjects) and survived until the Apple acquisition in
  late 1996. [Isaacson]

### The NeXT Diagnostic

NeXT is what happens when a founder takes the Apple-1985 reflexes (own the
whole stack, charge premium, ship beautiful, demand perfection) and applies
them to a market that cannot supply the corresponding volume. The reflexes
were not wrong at Apple in 1984; they were wrong at NeXT in 1989 because the
preconditions had changed.

Lessons Jobs absorbed (and applied at Apple after 1997):

- Vertical integration is a tool, not an identity. It works when volume,
  margin, and platform pull justify it, and destroys cash otherwise. [Schlender]
- Premium pricing requires either a category-defining product or a captive
  ecosystem. NeXT had neither; the iMac and iPod would have both. [Schlender]
- Perfectionism must be paired with shipping cadence. A late perfect product
  is a dead product. [Nerds 1996]
- The CEO's job includes saying no to the founder's instincts when those
  instincts are running the company into the ground. At NeXT, no one could.
  [Schlender]

## Pixar: The Patron Model

### What Jobs Bought

In February 1986 Jobs bought the computer graphics division of Lucasfilm for
about $5M plus a $5M capital commitment, renamed it Pixar, and inherited Ed
Catmull, John Lasseter, and a team that wanted to make computer-animated
films. Jobs initially thought he was buying a high-end imaging hardware
company. The Pixar Image Computer flopped. [Catmull; Pixar Story]

### What He Did Differently

This is the load-bearing part of the wilderness arc. At Pixar, Jobs was not
the auteur. Catmull ran the studio and the technical organization. Lasseter
ran the creative work. Jobs:

- Funded the company through years of losses (he eventually put in roughly
  $50M of his own capital). [Isaacson; Catmull]
- Negotiated the Disney distribution deals, including the renegotiation that
  followed *Toy Story*'s success. [Catmull]
- Stayed out of the story room. He did not direct films. He did not override
  Lasseter on creative calls. [Catmull; Pixar Story]
- Protected the culture from outside pressure, including from Disney.
  [Catmull]

Catmull's framing: Jobs was the financier and external champion; the creative
authority belonged to the filmmakers. When Jobs disagreed with a creative
decision, he generally lost the argument, and over time he stopped trying to
win those arguments. [Catmull]

### Why This Matters

The patron model is the inverse of the auteur model Jobs was running at NeXT.
At NeXT, his taste was the final word and the company suffered when his taste
was wrong about timing or market. At Pixar, his taste was a voice in the
conversation and the company thrived because the people closest to the craft
made the calls.

He did not generalize this everywhere. Back at Apple after 1997 he was still
intensely involved in product decisions. But he had learned where to be the
auteur (industrial design, OS direction, keynote storytelling) and where to
be the patron (Pixar-style creative work, deep engineering domains where
Avie Tevanian or Jon Rubinstein knew more than he did). [Schlender]

### When NOT To Be The Auteur

Apply the patron model when:

- The work is creative in a domain where iteration with peers produces better
  outcomes than top-down decree (film, novel-length writing, scientific
  research, deep engineering on unfamiliar systems).
- The people doing the work have more domain expertise than the leader.
- The leader's interventions are statistically making the work worse, not
  better.
- The team has a functioning internal critique culture (Pixar's "Braintrust"
  is the canonical example). [Catmull]

Apply the auteur model when:

- The work has a single coherent vision that fragments under committee.
- The leader has demonstrably better taste than the average team member in
  this specific domain.
- The decision is reversible cheaply and speed matters more than consensus.

Most leaders are auteurs in the wrong domain and patrons in none. The Jobs
maturation was learning to be both, in the right places.

## The Toy Story IPO And The Path Back

*Toy Story* released November 1995. Pixar IPO'd a week later (November 29,
1995); the stock more than doubled on day one and Jobs's stake was suddenly
worth over $1 billion. He was a billionaire from the company where he had been
the patron, not from the company where he had been the auteur. [Isaacson;
Pixar Story]

NeXT, meanwhile, was a struggling software company. In December 1996 Apple
agreed to acquire NeXT for about $429M plus stock, choosing NeXTSTEP over Be
Inc.'s BeOS as the foundation for the next-generation Mac OS. Jobs returned
as an advisor in early 1997, became interim CEO in September 1997 after Gil
Amelio was forced out, and the comeback began. [Isaacson; Schlender]

The path back was not straight. The order matters:

1. Pixar made him solvent and validated.
2. NeXT made him technically essential to Apple.
3. Apple's own crisis made him politically acceptable.

Take any of those out and the comeback does not happen.

## Voice: How Jobs Talked About Being Fired

Verbatim phrases worth recognizing (verify before quoting):

- "Sometimes life hits you in the head with a brick. Don't lose faith."
  [Stanford 2005]
- "I was a very public failure, and I even thought about running away from
  the valley." [Stanford 2005]
- "I'm convinced that the only thing that kept me going was that I loved
  what I did." [Stanford 2005]
- On NeXT in 1995: "I'm an optimist in the sense that I believe humans are
  noble and honorable, and some of them are really smart." [Lost Interview,
  paraphrase]
- On the firing in 1995, while still at NeXT: he framed it as painful but
  said the work itself, not the title, was what had mattered. [Lost Interview]

Tonal note for advisor use: in 1995 Jobs was still describing the firing with
real residual heat. By 2005 he had the redemption-arc voice. A user in their
own 1985 will sound like 1995 Jobs, not 2005 Jobs. Do not push them to
perform gratitude they have not earned yet.

## Stories Worth Knowing

### The Firing Itself (May 1985)

Sculley took the question of Jobs's role to the board after months of
escalating conflict over the Mac division. The board sided with Sculley
unanimously. Jobs was given a corner office in a separate building (he called
it "Siberia"), kept the chairman title, and was effectively done. He resigned
in September 1985 and announced he was starting NeXT. [Isaacson]

### The NeXT Cube Launch (October 1988)

Jobs rented Davies Symphony Hall in San Francisco and gave a three-hour
demonstration. The product was beautiful, expensive, and not yet shipping in
volume. The reception was reverent; the sales numbers that followed were not.
The launch is a case study in how a perfect demo does not equal a perfect
business. [Isaacson; Nerds 1996]

### The Pixar Acquisition (February 1986)

Lucas wanted to sell the computer division for $30M; Jobs paid roughly $5M
plus capital commitments. The deal closed in February 1986. For most of the
next decade, Pixar lost money and Jobs personally funded payroll multiple
times. He came close to selling it more than once, including to Microsoft.
[Isaacson; Catmull]

### Toy Story And The IPO (November 1995)

The film opened to $29M its first weekend, eventually grossed $373M
worldwide, and validated computer animation as a feature-film medium. The IPO
followed a week later and made Jobs richer than the original Apple shares
ever had. [Pixar Story; Isaacson]

### The Return (1996-1997)

Apple acquired NeXT in December 1996. Jobs was officially an advisor, but
within months was effectively running product strategy. By July 1997 he had
engineered Amelio's removal; by September he was interim CEO ("iCEO"). At the
1997 Macworld he announced the Microsoft investment and patent
cross-license, ending the Microsoft-Apple cold war. The actual product
turnaround (iMac, OS X, iPod) followed. [Isaacson; Schlender]

## How To Apply: User Has Been Fired Or Sidelined

Sequence the conversation roughly as follows:

1. **Acknowledge the heat.** Do not skip to redemption. The Stanford voice is
   a 2005 voice. The user is in 1985.
2. **Separate identity from operating role.** "What had been the focus of my
   entire adult life was gone." [Stanford 2005] The grief is real; it is also
   data about how concentrated the user's identity had become in one role.
3. **Find the asset that survives the fall.** Jobs left Apple with: capital,
   reputation among engineers, taste, network, and the ability to recruit. He
   did not leave with: distribution, a customer base, or a product team. Ask
   the user what they actually own that the firing did not take.
4. **Run two laboratories, not one.** Jobs ran NeXT and Pixar in parallel.
   The auteur lab and the patron lab. If the user is putting all of their
   wilderness energy into a single bet (usually a defensive replay of the
   thing they were fired from), suggest a second lab in a different mode.
5. **Pick the patron bet carefully.** Pixar worked because Catmull and
   Lasseter were already excellent and Jobs's job was to fund and protect
   them. The patron bet requires real talent on the inside. It is not "I'll
   be a passive investor"; it is "I'll be the external champion of people
   doing work I respect."
6. **Expect the wilderness to last longer than feels reasonable.** Twelve
   years from firing to comeback. Pixar lost money for nine of those years.
   The IPO window opened on a single film's success. The user should plan
   for a wilderness measured in years, not quarters.
7. **Do not perform the comeback.** Jobs's 1985-1995 was not a public
   redemption tour. It was mostly losing money on a workstation nobody
   bought. The redemption framing is retrospective. Real-time, the work is
   the work.

## How To Apply: User Is In A Long Failing Bet

This is the NeXT-shaped question. The product is excellent, the team is
loyal, and the market is not buying. When does excellence justify continued
funding and when is it sunk-cost theater?

NeXT diagnostic questions:

- Is the failure about timing or about market existence? NeXTSTEP was right
  about object-oriented development; the workstation market was wrong about
  volume. The software bet survived; the hardware bet did not.
- What part of the work would still be valuable if the current product line
  died tomorrow? At NeXT, the answer was the OS. At Pixar pre-*Toy Story*,
  the answer was the rendering pipeline and the people. Both bets had a
  surviving asset.
- Is the founder defending the integrated product because integration is
  strategically right, or because integration is identity? Jobs killed NeXT
  hardware in 1993. The identity was painful to give up; the math was
  obvious by 1991.
- Is there a path where the technical work becomes essential to a larger
  player? NeXT-as-software became the path back to Apple. Pixar's pipeline
  became Disney's animation future. Both terminal states involved being
  acquired or partnered with the dominant player in the category.

If the answer to all four is "no surviving asset, identity-driven, no
acquirer path," the bet is closer to sunk-cost than to wilderness. End it.

## Failure Mode: Not Every Wilderness Becomes A Comeback

The Jobs arc is survivorship bias. For every founder who came back from a
firing to run their company at peak, there are many who did not. The lesson
of the wilderness is not "you will return triumphant." The lesson is what you
do *during* it.

What Jobs did during the wilderness:

- Kept working. NeXT and Pixar were both demanding full-time companies. He
  did not take a sabbatical. [Isaacson; Schlender]
- Built technical assets that survived even when the business plan did not
  (NeXTSTEP, Pixar's pipeline). [Schlender]
- Funded a patron bet in parallel to the auteur bet, hedging across two
  fundamentally different operating models. [Catmull]
- Got married (1991) and started a family. The Schlender thesis is that
  Laurene Powell Jobs and fatherhood softened him in ways that mattered for
  the comeback. [Schlender; Isaacson]
- Maintained relationships even with people he had fought (the Sculley-era
  Apple board, Bill Gates, John Lasseter under contractual stress). The
  relationship capital was not depleted in public feuds. [Schlender]

What Jobs did *not* do:

- Wait passively for vindication.
- Treat the firing as proof he had been right all along.
- Relitigate the firing in public for a decade.
- Assume the comeback was owed to him.

Failure-mode trap: the user reads the Stanford speech, identifies with the
firing, and adopts the comeback expectation without doing the actual work of
the wilderness. The lesson is the work, not the entitlement.

Advisor move: when a user starts to sound entitled to a 2.0, point out that
Jobs's 2.0 required (a) an excellent technical asset Apple needed, (b) a
separate company that made him solvent and credible, (c) twelve years of
shipping, and (d) Apple being in genuine crisis. Subtract any one and there
is no return.

## Decision Questions

- What asset survives the fall? Capital, skill, network, reputation, IP,
  taste, or recruiting power?
- What is the auteur bet (your vision, your call) and what is the patron
  bet (someone else's craft, your support)?
- Where is the user in the timeline: month two, year two, or year ten?
- Is the current long bet a NeXT (technically excellent, commercially failing,
  with a surviving asset) or a sunk-cost replay of a fight already lost?
- Who is the user's Catmull or Lasseter, the person whose work they should
  be funding rather than directing?
- What relationships from the previous chapter need to be preserved for a
  return path that does not yet exist?
- What part of the previous role was identity rather than work, and can be
  put down?
- If no comeback ever arrives, what would still make the wilderness work
  worth doing?

## Traps

- **Stanford-voice trap:** Performing the redemption arc before it has been
  earned. The 2005 voice is retrospective. In real time, the work is grief
  and shipping.
- **Auteur-everywhere trap:** Applying the NeXT operating model to a Pixar
  problem. Directing creatives who know more than you do.
- **Patron-everywhere trap:** Applying the Pixar operating model to a NeXT
  problem. Refusing to make the call when the call is yours.
- **Comeback-entitlement trap:** Assuming the firing automatically buys a
  2.0. It does not. The 2.0 is bought by the wilderness work.
- **Sunk-cost dressed as conviction:** Funding the failing bet because
  giving up would be giving up, not because the math has improved.
- **Identity-integration trap:** Refusing to unbundle the integrated product
  even when the volume to support it does not exist.
- **Public-feud trap:** Relitigating the firing for years. Jobs largely did
  not, and the relationship capital paid off when Apple needed him.
- **Single-lab trap:** Putting all wilderness energy into one bet (usually
  a defensive replay) instead of running two labs in different modes.

## Advisor Moves

- Distinguish 1985 voice from 2005 voice. Match the user's actual timeline.
- Map the user's surviving assets explicitly: capital, skill, network,
  reputation, IP, recruiting power.
- Identify the auteur bet and the patron bet. If only one exists, suggest a
  second lab in the other mode.
- For long failing bets, run the NeXT diagnostic: surviving asset, identity
  vs. strategy, acquirer path, timing vs. market existence.
- Protect relationship capital. Do not let the user burn bridges that the
  return path will require.
- Hold the line on patience. Twelve years is the reference timeline, not
  twelve months.
- Refuse to validate entitlement to a comeback. Validate the work that
  produces one.
- Watch for life-stage maturation (partnership, family, longer time
  horizons) as part of the arc, not separate from it.

## Use With Care

- Do not recommend that every fired founder start two companies. Jobs had
  capital from the Apple IPO. Most users do not.
- Do not romanticize the wilderness. It was twelve years of losing money on
  one company and patron-funding another. The redemption arc is the
  retrospective frame.
- Do not generalize the patron model into "let your team do everything."
  Jobs was the patron at Pixar and the auteur at Apple-after-1997. Both
  modes, in the right domains.
- Do not assume the user's wilderness becomes a comeback. The lesson is the
  work during it, not the return.
- Verify quotes against original sources before putting them on the record.
  The Stanford 2005 transcript is public; *The Lost Interview* is on home
  video; Schlender, Isaacson, and Catmull are in print.
