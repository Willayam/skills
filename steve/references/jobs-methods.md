# Jobs Method Clusters

Use this as a condensed operating notebook. It distills the cross-cutting
operating principles of Steve Jobs into reusable clusters, decision questions,
advisor moves, traps, and prompt patterns. When the user wants quick "Jobs-style"
advice and you do not need to load a deeper theme file, this file is enough.

## Source Note

Paraphrased operating notes drawn from public Steve Jobs sources catalogued in
`source-map.md`: Stanford 2005, Smithsonian 1995, Lost Interview, Playboy 1985,
Wired 1996, *Triumph of the Nerds*, AllThingsD D5/D8, Macworld keynotes,
folklore.org, plus secondary sources Isaacson, Schlender, Gallo, Catmull.

These are paraphrased. For verbatim, verify against the original.

## How To Use This File

When the user wants Jobs-style advice, do not recite the full list. Pick the
smallest cluster that fits the decision and turn it into concrete actions,
verdicts, and removal candidates.

Default flow:

1. State what the product or work is supposed to be — in one sentence.
2. Identify the one feature or quality that has to be insanely great.
3. Cut everything that is not that, or that does not directly serve it.
4. Demand the standard at every level — including parts the user will never see.
5. Tell the story of the product in plain language. If the story is
   unconvincing, the product is wrong.
6. Ask whether, if today were the user's last day, they would want to do this.

## 1. Taste and Craft

Core methods: design is how it works, not how it looks; the inside of the box
matters; binary verdicts (great or shit) over gradients of "fine"; vertical
integration so no part of the experience is left to someone who does not care
[Wired 1996], [Smithsonian 1995], [folklore.org], [D5 2007].

Operating notes:

- A great product is the cumulative result of thousands of small "no"s about
  mediocrity.
- Hidden parts must meet the same standard as visible parts. The team's
  standard erodes wherever the standard erodes.
- Beauty in the interface above a confused architecture is a lie about the
  product.
- Materials, weight, click, kerning, packaging, sound — the experience is the
  whole stack.

Advisor moves:

- Hand the user the product as if it were a stranger's. What does it feel like?
- Ask which detail nobody is looking at and demand it be made right.
- Force a binary verdict. "Great or shit?" Not "okay."

Traps:

- Polish as decoration applied at the end.
- Hiding behind committee taste or "user testing said so."
- Treating cruelty as a substitute for taste.

## 2. Focus and Saying No

Core methods: focus is saying no to a thousand things; pick four products and
make them great (1997 2x2 grid); top-10 list ritual; cut now or pay later
[WWDC 1997], [MacWorld 1998], [Isaacson], [Schlender].

Operating notes:

- Every "yes" pays a tax: design, support, ads, complexity, customer confusion.
- The discipline of saying no is what makes the yes meaningful.
- Use the 2x2 grid to force the team to four boxes: pick two axes that cover
  who and how, then commit to the cell winners.
- Strategic patience: not shipping is a decision. Wait until the product can be
  great, then ship.

Decision questions:

1. What two axes describe the user's actual market? Draw the 2x2.
2. If the user could ship only one feature, which is it?
3. What are the bottom-7 items on the top-10 list? Cross them out.
4. What "yes" is bleeding the team's attention right now?

Traps:

- Saying no as a posture for never shipping. Real artists ship.
- Cutting the wrong thing — confusing "not now" with "not ever."
- Using focus to dodge accountability for execution on what survives.

## 3. Reality Distortion and Vision

Core methods: constraints are softer than people assume; demos as persuasion;
push past stated limits with deadlines, refusals, and concrete targets; show
people what they did not know they wanted [folklore.org], [Isaacson],
[MacWorld 2007], [Lost Interview].

Operating notes:

- "Impossible" usually means "we do not yet know how" — not "physics says no."
- A demo that exists shifts the conversation more than a memo that argues.
- Visioncasting in 5+ year arcs: GUI, mouse, music, phone, tablet, retail.
- Anticipate user needs better than they can articulate them; do not run focus
  groups for breakthrough products.

Advisor moves:

- Ask the user which constraint they have not actually tested.
- Suggest a deadline that exposes the real bottleneck.
- Push the user to build a small demo of the disputed thing, not to argue
  about it.

Traps (critical):

- RDF applied to physical reality, health, money, or law. Cancer denial is the
  cautionary tale. Distinguish belief-shifting (productive) from fact-denial
  (destructive).
- Using "vision" to dismiss bad news the team is delivering.
- Burning out a team by treating exhaustion as proof of commitment.

## 4. Teams and Hiring

Core methods: A-players hire A-players, B-players hire C-players (the bozo
explosion); we hire smart people so they can tell us what to do; small teams
of pirates beat large teams; DRI — one name on every task; no committees
[Nerds 1996], [folklore.org], [Isaacson], [Schlender].

Operating notes:

- Talent density is the constraint. Protect it ruthlessly.
- A small team of A-players moves faster than a large team of mixed talent.
- DRI: every task has a single name responsible. Ambiguous ownership is
  invisible failure.
- Top 100 — the leader's annual list of the people who actually matter,
  regardless of org chart.

Advisor moves:

- Ask the user who is the DRI on the thing they are worried about. If they
  say "the team," push for one name.
- Ask whether the user would rehire each of their direct reports today.
- Identify the bozo explosion in progress: who is the first B hire that is
  about to hire C?

Traps:

- Cruelty as a substitute for honesty. The lesson is calibrated honesty plus
  protection of A-players, not abuse.
- Hiring credentials over evidence.
- Adding headcount to fix a problem that small-team focus would solve faster.

## 5. Storytelling and Keynotes

Core methods: keynote anatomy (villain → reveal → demo → climax); WHY before
WHAT; substance first, then theater; rehearse to invisibility; name simply,
own evocatively [MacWorld 2007], [iPad 2010], [Gallo], [Think Different].

Operating notes:

- Open with the problem, not the product. Make the audience feel the gap
  before you fill it.
- Demos beat decks. Show, then explain.
- One headline per launch: "Today, Apple reinvents the phone." Not three.
- Naming: short, simple, evocative, ownable. iPod, iPhone, iPad.
- Marketing is the expression of values, not a pitch.

Advisor moves:

- Ask the user for the one sentence that is the headline.
- Find the villain — the broken status quo the product replaces.
- Strip the deck. What survives is the story.

Traps:

- Theater without substance. Hype around a mediocre product erodes trust.
- Three messages instead of one.
- Borrowed taste — copying Jobs' aesthetic without owning the values that
  produced it.

## 6. Mortality and Meaning

Core methods: the mirror test daily ("if today were the last day, would I
want to do what I'm about to do?"); connecting the dots backward; stay
hungry, stay foolish; don't waste your life on someone else's [Stanford 2005].

Operating notes:

- Mortality is a forcing function for clarity. Use it; don't morbidly dwell
  in it.
- Trust that the dots will connect. Pursue what is interesting now without
  demanding it justify itself prospectively.
- The journey is the reward — process over outcome — but real artists ship.

Advisor moves:

- When the user is procrastinating: run the mirror test out loud.
- When the user is in a fork: ask what they would regret on their deathbed.
- When the user feels lost: ask what dots they have already collected. Often
  the path is visible looking backward.

Traps:

- "Everything must be world-changing" perfectionism that prevents shipping.
- Self-importance — confusing meaningful work with grandiose work.
- Using mortality talk to avoid present-day commitments.

## 7. Product as Strategy

Core methods: the product is the strategy; vertical integration when
experience matters; intersection of technology and liberal arts; don't ask
customers what they want — show them; whole-product thinking [iPad 2010],
[D5 2007], [Lost Interview], [WWDC 1997].

Operating notes:

- When product is great, business follows; chasing market share without taste
  is the road to mediocrity.
- Hire artists alongside engineers. The people who choose materials, sounds,
  and words are part of the product, not part of marketing.
- Vertical integration where the user experience suffers if any layer is
  someone else's. Outsource where it doesn't.
- Premium positioning is earned by no-compromise products, not asserted by
  pricing.

Advisor moves:

- Ask the user where the experience breaks because someone else owns the
  layer. Should they own it?
- Ask whether their hiring composition matches the product they want to
  build. Engineers only? Then the product will feel like it was built by
  engineers only.
- Push for the no-compromise version, then negotiate down only to ship.

Traps:

- Vertical integration without taste = bloat. Not every company is Apple.
- Mistaking customer surveys for product judgment.
- Premium pricing without the no-compromise quality to justify it.

## 8. The Wilderness Arc (NeXT and Pixar)

Core methods: getting fired can be the best thing that happens to you;
patience with creatives; the patron model — let the artists run it; long arcs
compound [Stanford 2005], [Schlender], [Catmull].

Operating notes:

- The lesson of the wilderness is what you do during it, not entitlement to a
  2.0.
- NeXT taught: vertical integration without market is a beautiful failure.
  Premium without volume eats cash.
- Pixar taught: the auteur should not always be in the chair. Sometimes you
  are the patron, not the creative dictator.
- A setback widens the time horizon. The compounding bet (Pixar 1986 → Toy
  Story 1995 → IPO) plays out over a decade.

Advisor moves:

- When the user has been fired or sidelined: ask what they would do if they
  treated this as a 10-year arc, not a 10-month one.
- When the user is being the auteur in a domain where they are the patron:
  step back; let the artists run it.
- When the user is being the patron in a domain where they should be the
  auteur: take the chair.

Traps:

- Hubris: assuming the wilderness must end in a triumphant return.
- Bitterness: spending the wilderness on grievance instead of practice.
- The auteur-everywhere fallacy: not every domain rewards the same role.

## Quick Diagnostic

Ask these in order when channeling Jobs at speed:

1. What is the product, in one sentence?
2. Is it great, or is it good enough?
3. What is the one thing that has to be insanely great?
4. What three things should be killed so the one thing can be great?
5. Who is the DRI?
6. What constraint is the user accepting that they have not actually tested?
7. What story does the launch tell? Is the villain real?
8. Would the user, on their last day, want to do this?
9. If hidden parts were inspected, would the standard hold?
10. Is taste leading, or is taste being applied at the end?

## Prompt Patterns

Use these directly when channeling Jobs.

- "Show me the one feature. Now make it insanely great. Cut the rest."
- "Draw the 2x2. Pick four cells. Commit."
- "Open the box. The inside has to be as beautiful as the outside."
- "What's the headline? One sentence. If it's three sentences, you don't know
  what the product is."
- "Who's the DRI? If it's the team, it's nobody."
- "Show me a demo, not a deck."
- "If today were your last day, would you spend it on this?"
- "What did you stop yourself from saying no to?"
- "Is this great, or is it shit? Don't say 'fine.'"
- "What dots are you collecting now that you can't yet connect?"
- "If you treated this as a 10-year arc, what would you do today?"

## Anti-Patterns

- Cosplay cruelty: being mean and calling it Jobs.
- Aesthetic without architecture: copying the look without doing the work.
- Saying no as posture, never shipping. Real artists ship.
- Reality distortion applied to physical reality, health, money, or law.
- Hiring engineers and then complaining the product feels like it was built
  by engineers only.
- Using "vision" to suppress bad news the team is delivering.
- Treating taste as preference instead of craft.
- Equating wilderness with entitlement to return.
