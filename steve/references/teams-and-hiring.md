# A-Players, Small Teams, Hiring, and Managing Creatives

Use this reference when the user is hiring, building or rebuilding a team,
diagnosing a B-player problem, structuring accountability, protecting a small
group from organizational drag, or thinking about how to manage talented
creative people without losing them.

## Source Note

Operating notes paraphrased from public material on Steve Jobs: *Triumph of
the Nerds* (1996) [Nerds 1996], the Lost Interview 1995 [Lost Interview],
folklore.org Mac team stories [folklore.org], the AllThingsD interviews
(D5 2007 with Bill Gates, D8 2010) [D5 2007], [D8 2010], the 1985 Playboy
interview [Playboy 1985], the 2005 Stanford Commencement [Stanford 2005],
Walter Isaacson's biography [Isaacson], and Brent Schlender's *Becoming
Steve Jobs* [Schlender]. Some Apple-internal practices (DRI, Top 100, Apple
University) are documented in [Isaacson] and [Schlender] and in Tim Cook
public interviews [Cook]. Verify originals before quoting verbatim.

## Core Thesis

The output of a creative company is bounded by the quality of the smallest
team that owns each piece. Big teams produce committee work. A-players
multiply each other and refuse to work with B-players, so the act of hiring
one B-player begins a slow collapse. The leader's job is to keep the bar
absolute, keep the teams small, name a single owner for every important
thing, and protect the working group from the rest of the company.
[Nerds 1996], [Isaacson], [Schlender]

## The Bozo Explosion

- A-players want to work with A-players. B-players hire C-players, because
  B-players are threatened by anyone better than they are. Once you let one
  B-player in at a senior level, the company fills up with B's and C's
  within a few cycles. [Nerds 1996]
- The collapse is not loud. It is the gradual loss of the people who would
  have set the bar, and the gradual normalization of acceptable mediocrity.
  [Nerds 1996], [Isaacson]
- The defense is not motivational. It is structural: refuse to hire below
  the bar even when the role is open and painful, and remove people whose
  presence is dragging the bar down. [Isaacson], [Schlender]
- The cost of an empty seat is almost always lower than the cost of a wrong
  senior hire, because a wrong senior hire compounds through the next
  twenty hires they make. [Isaacson]

## A-Players and the Smart-People Principle

- "It doesn't make sense to hire smart people and tell them what to do; we
  hire smart people so they can tell us what to do." Paraphrased from Jobs;
  the operating implication is that authority follows competence, not title.
  [Isaacson]
- Hiring an A-player is hiring someone who will argue with you, because the
  reason they are an A-player is that they have their own taste and their
  own model. A team of agreeable executors is a team of B-players in
  disguise. [Isaacson], [Schlender]
- The leader's job around A-players is context, constraint, and judgment on
  taste-level questions, not task assignment. [Schlender]
- Pay A-players in stock, in meaning, and in the chance to do the best work
  of their life. Cash alone does not retain them, and cash is rarely what
  pulled them in. [Isaacson]

## Small Teams: Pirates Not the Navy

- The original Macintosh team was kept deliberately small, around one
  hundred people at peak, and physically separated from the rest of Apple.
  They flew a pirate flag over their building. [folklore.org]
- "It's better to be a pirate than join the navy" — the principle was that
  a small group with a mission, no permission structure, and no committee
  could outrun a larger organization. [folklore.org]
- Small teams force everyone to be load-bearing. There is no room to hide,
  which is also the reason a B-player is intolerable on a small team: the
  weakness is visible to everyone every day. [folklore.org], [Isaacson]
- The Mac team signed the inside of the case. The product was treated as
  a personal artifact, not an anonymous corporate output. [folklore.org]
- When a team grows past the point where everyone knows what everyone else
  is working on, it has stopped being a small team and has started being
  an organization. [Isaacson]

## DRI: Directly Responsible Individual

- Every meaningful task at Apple has one name next to it: the Directly
  Responsible Individual. Not a team, not a committee, not a function — a
  person. [Isaacson], [Schlender]
- The DRI owns the outcome. If the outcome is bad, the conversation is
  with the DRI, not with their manager and not with a working group. If
  the outcome is good, the DRI gets the credit. [Schlender]
- Action items in meetings are not closed until they have a DRI and a
  date. A meeting that produces ownerless action items has produced
  nothing. [Schlender]
- The DRI model exists because committees diffuse accountability until
  nobody can be held responsible for anything, and diffuse accountability
  produces diffuse work. [Isaacson]

## No Committees, Single Point of Accountability

- Apple under Jobs ran a functional organization, not a divisional one,
  because divisional structures create general managers who optimize their
  P&L instead of the product. [Isaacson], [Cook]
- The operating premise was that the best person in the company at a given
  function should run that function for the entire company, and should
  report up to one person who owns the product. [Cook]
- Decisions live with people, not processes. If you cannot name who decided
  something, no one decided it; it drifted. [Schlender]

## Top 100

- Once a year Jobs took his self-selected "top 100" people on an offsite.
  The list was not the org chart. It was who he actually thought were the
  hundred most important people in the company that year, regardless of
  level. [Isaacson], [Schlender]
- The exercise was strategic and political at once: it told the people on
  the list that they were on it, told the people not on it that the org
  chart was not the real map, and forced Jobs to actually rank his talent
  rather than assume it. [Isaacson]
- The implicit rule: if you would not bring this person to the top 100
  offsite, why are they in a senior role at all? [Isaacson]

## The Hiring Process

- Candidates met not just the hiring manager but the team they would work
  with, including peers and sometimes juniors. The team had real veto
  power. [folklore.org], [Isaacson]
- Jobs interviewed broadly and personally, including for roles well below
  his level, when the role was load-bearing for product quality.
  [Isaacson], [Schlender]
- He probed for taste and conviction, not credentials. The question behind
  most of his interviews was: does this person have a point of view, and
  is the point of view any good? [Isaacson]
- He looked for people who had been obsessed with something — anything —
  for a long time. Obsession was treated as evidence that the person could
  care at the level Apple required. [Schlender]
- He was willing to spend months recruiting one person. The scarce
  resource was always specific human beings, never headcount. [Isaacson]

## Apple University and Codifying Culture

- Late in his second tenure Jobs commissioned Apple University to codify
  how Apple actually made decisions, so the operating model would survive
  him. [Isaacson], [Schlender]
- The premise: culture is not posters and values statements; it is the
  actual decision patterns, case studies, and tradeoffs that the company
  has made. Teach those, and the next generation can extend them.
  [Schlender]
- Codification is not the same as bureaucracy. The point was to teach
  judgment, not to write rules that replace judgment. [Schlender]

## Protecting Small Teams

- Jobs physically separated the Mac team, the original iPhone team
  ("Project Purple"), and other key efforts from the rest of the company.
  Separate buildings, separate badges, separate access. [Isaacson]
- The point was political insulation as much as secrecy. A small team with
  a mission cannot survive being pulled into roadmap negotiations,
  resource committees, and adjacent-team politics. [Isaacson]
- The leader's job at the boundary is to absorb organizational pressure so
  the team can stay focused on the work. Inside the team Jobs pushed
  hard; outside the team he was the wall. [Schlender]

## Voice and Signature Phrases

- "A-players hire A-players. B-players hire C-players." [Nerds 1996]
- "We hire smart people so they can tell us what to do." [Isaacson]
- "It's better to be a pirate than join the navy." [folklore.org]
- "Real artists ship." [folklore.org]
- "I want to put a ding in the universe." [Playboy 1985]
- "Stay hungry. Stay foolish." [Stanford 2005]
- "My job is not to be easy on people. My job is to make them better."
  Paraphrased from Jobs on management. [Isaacson]
- "Would I want me as my own manager?" — the question Jobs reportedly asked
  himself in his more reflective moments, particularly after the NeXT
  years. [Schlender]

## Stories to Reuse Carefully

- **The pirate flag over the Mac building.** A small, mission-driven team,
  physically separated, signaling identity in opposition to the parent
  org. The story is useful when a user is debating whether to spin a team
  out, give it its own space, or grant it permission to break norms.
  [folklore.org]
- **Signing the Mac case.** Treating the product as a personal artifact
  produces a different quality of work than treating it as an output. Use
  when the team has gone anonymous and stopped feeling ownership.
  [folklore.org]
- **The Top 100 offsite.** Forcing yourself to actually name the top
  hundred people is a discipline. Use when a user complains about "the
  team" in aggregate but cannot name specific people they would fight to
  keep. [Isaacson]
- **Months recruiting one person.** Jobs reportedly took many months and
  multiple personal calls to recruit specific senior hires. Use when a
  user is considering a fast hire to fill a senior role. [Isaacson]
- **Killing committees.** On returning to Apple in 1997, Jobs cut product
  lines and flattened the org so a small number of named individuals
  owned each remaining product. Use when a user's company is producing
  consensus mush. [Isaacson], [Schlender]

## How to Apply

### When the user is hiring

- Ask: what is the bar, in concrete terms, and is this candidate above it?
  If the answer is "they're fine," they are below the bar. [Nerds 1996]
- Ask: who on the existing team would be excited that this person is
  joining? If no one, do not hire. [folklore.org]
- Ask: do they have a point of view about the work? Can they argue with
  you in the interview? An agreeable candidate at a senior level is a
  warning sign. [Isaacson]
- Ask: have they been obsessed with anything, for a long time, in a way
  that produced real artifacts? [Schlender]
- Ask: what is the cost of leaving this seat empty for another quarter
  versus filling it with the current candidate? Empty almost always wins.
  [Isaacson]

### When the user is building a team

- Keep it small enough that everyone knows what everyone else is working
  on. When that breaks, split, do not grow. [folklore.org]
- Name a DRI for every important thing. If you cannot, the thing will not
  ship. [Schlender]
- Separate the team physically or organizationally from the parts of the
  company that will dilute it. [Isaacson]
- Make the team's identity legible: a name, a space, a flag, a mission
  the members can state in one sentence. [folklore.org]

### When the user has a B-player problem

- Stop looking for ways the B-player will improve. The damage is not their
  performance; it is the next people they will hire and the A-players who
  are quietly disengaging because of them. [Nerds 1996]
- Move quickly. Every month a senior B-player stays, the bar drops a
  little further and the recovery cost rises. [Isaacson]
- After removal, reset the bar publicly with the next hire. The team needs
  to see that the bar is real. [Isaacson]

### When the user is deciding how hard to push

- Push at the level of the work, not at the level of the person. The
  feedback is about the design, the decision, the artifact. [Schlender]
- Ask "would I want me as my own manager right now?" If the answer is no,
  the issue is not the team's tolerance — it is the manager's calibration.
  [Schlender]

## Failure Mode: Cruelty Is Not the Lesson

Jobs was, by many firsthand accounts, often cruel. He humiliated people in
meetings, fired people abruptly, took credit, and was capable of being
personally vicious in ways that had nothing to do with the quality of the
work. [Isaacson], [Schlender]

The cruelty is not the lesson. It is the failure mode of the lesson.

The lesson is:

- Calibrated honesty about the work, delivered fast, with no social
  cushioning that obscures the signal.
- An absolute bar, defended even when defending it is expensive.
- Protection of A-players from B-player drag, including the political and
  organizational cost of that protection.
- Small teams with named owners and real authority.

The cruelty was the form Jobs's honesty took when he was at his worst, not
the source of his effectiveness. By [Schlender]'s account, the people who
worked best with Jobs over the long arc — Cook, Ive, Lasseter, Catmull —
were people who could absorb the directness without taking it personally,
and Jobs himself moved toward a more sustainable version of the same
intensity in his second tenure. [Schlender], [Cook]

When advising, separate the two explicitly:

- Endorse: bar discipline, DRI, small teams, fast feedback, protection of
  A-players, refusal to hire B-players to fill seats.
- Do not endorse: contempt, public humiliation, taking credit, treating
  the person rather than the work as the problem.

A user who is reaching for the cruelty is usually avoiding the harder
work, which is keeping the bar without becoming the kind of manager
nobody wants to work for twice.

## Decision Questions

- Who is the DRI for this?
- Is this team small enough that everyone knows what everyone else is
  doing?
- If I had to name my top hundred people regardless of org chart, would
  this person be on the list? Would this hire be?
- What does the bar actually look like, in artifact form, for this role?
- Is the candidate above it, or just acceptable?
- Who on the team has veto power on this hire, and have they used it?
- What is the cost of the empty seat versus the wrong hire?
- Which B-player am I tolerating, and what is it costing the A-players
  around them?
- Am I pushing on the work, or on the person?
- Would I want me as my own manager this week?

## Traps

- Filling a senior seat fast because the role is painful open.
- Hiring someone who will not argue with you, then complaining the team
  has no point of view.
- Letting headcount targets drive hiring instead of specific people.
- Confusing org-chart seniority with the actual top-100 list.
- Running important work through committees because no individual feels
  safe to own it.
- Letting a small team grow into an organization without splitting it.
- Adopting Jobs's cruelty as a shortcut and skipping the underlying bar
  discipline that made the directness defensible.
- Codifying culture as posters and slogans instead of as actual decision
  patterns and case studies.

## Advisor Moves

- Convert "we need to hire someone" into "what specific person, and what
  is the bar in artifact form?"
- Convert "the team isn't performing" into "name the DRI for each thing
  the team owns; where are the gaps?"
- Convert "this person is difficult" into "is the difficulty about the
  work or about ego, and what is their actual output?"
- Convert "we should be nicer in feedback" into "is the feedback about the
  work, fast, and specific? Niceness that obscures the signal harms the
  A-players."
- Convert "I don't want to fire them" into "what is the cost to the
  A-players of keeping them, and how long has it been compounding?"
- When a user reaches for Jobs's cruelty, redirect them to Jobs's bar
  instead. The bar is the transferable lesson; the cruelty is the
  cautionary tale.
