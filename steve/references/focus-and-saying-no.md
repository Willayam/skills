# Focus and Saying No

Use this reference when the user is over-scoped, drowning in features, unable to
cut, asking which of ten things to ship, defending a sprawling roadmap, adding
"just one more" thing, or trying to be everything to everyone. Also use when the
user needs permission to kill a beloved project, line item, or product.

## Core Thesis

Focus is not the same as concentration. Focus is the active, repeated decision
to refuse the thousand good ideas in order to make the few right ones great.
Every "yes" is a tax on the things that already deserve to exist. Pruning is
the price of excellence. Strategic patience — the willingness to wait — is part
of the discipline. [WWDC 1997, D5 2007, Isaacson]

## Core Principles

- Focus means saying no, not saying yes. Saying no to a thousand good ideas is
  how the remaining few become great. [WWDC 1997, Isaacson]
- The "good ideas" you reject are usually genuinely good in isolation. The
  question is whether they belong inside this product, this quarter, this
  company, this life. [WWDC 1997]
- A product line should fit on a napkin. If you cannot draw it from memory, the
  customer cannot understand it either. [MacWorld 1998, Isaacson]
- Pruning makes the surviving branches stronger. Cutting is an act of care for
  the things that remain. [Isaacson, Schlender]
- The decision tax of every "yes" is paid by the team, the user, and the
  product, not by the person who said yes. [Isaacson]
- Strategic patience is part of focus. Waiting until the technology, market, or
  team is ready is a decision, not passivity. [D8 2010, Schlender]
- Saying no protects the soul of the product. Saying yes too often turns a
  product into a feature list. [D5 2007, Isaacson]
- Real focus requires a willingness to disappoint smart, sincere people whose
  ideas are good but not central. [Isaacson]

## The 2x2 Grid as a Tool

When Jobs returned to Apple in 1997, the product line had grown to dozens of
overlapping models with confusing names (Performa, Quadra, Centris, LC, and
several Power Mac variants). He drew a 2x2 grid on a whiteboard and told the
team Apple would now ship four computers: one in each quadrant. [MacWorld 1998,
Isaacson, Schlender]

```
                   Consumer        Pro
                ┌──────────────┬──────────────┐
        Desktop │   iMac       │  Power Mac   │
                ├──────────────┼──────────────┤
       Portable │   iBook      │  PowerBook   │
                └──────────────┴──────────────┘
```

The lesson is the structure, not the labels. The grid forces three commitments:

1. **Two axes that capture the real customer split.** The axes have to be the
   distinctions the buyer actually uses to pick. For Apple in 1997 it was
   user type and form factor.
2. **One product per cell.** Not a family, not a "good/better/best" ladder
   inside the cell. One thing the team can make great. [Isaacson]
3. **Everything outside the grid is a candidate for deletion.** Printers,
   servers, the Newton, OpenDoc, eMate, and most peripherals went to zero.
   [Isaacson, Apple history]

How to apply it to a user's product or work:

- Name the two axes that actually drive the choice for your user (e.g.,
  individual vs team, free vs paid, mobile vs desktop, technical vs
  non-technical). Reject vanity axes that flatter your roadmap.
- Place every current offering in a cell. If two offerings share a cell, one
  of them is going to die or be merged.
- Mark every cell that is currently empty *and* strategically required. Decide
  whether to fill it or accept the gap on purpose.
- Put everything outside the grid on the deletion list. Defend each survivor
  with a named owner and a reason. Apply the same template to features,
  campaigns, hires, or personal commitments — not just products.
- If the grid has more than four cells, you probably do not have focus; you
  have a spreadsheet.

## Stories

### 1997 Return and the Product Massacre

When Jobs returned in 1997, Apple was weeks from insolvency and shipping a
chaotic catalog. He killed roughly 70% of the products, ended the Mac clone
licensing program, shut down Newton, OpenDoc, Cyberdog, eMate, printers, and
most peripherals, and consolidated the remaining computers onto the 2x2 grid.
The cuts hurt loyal customers and ended internal careers. The surviving line
got resources, focus, and the iMac launched the next year. [Isaacson,
Schlender, Apple history]

Derived lessons:

- A turnaround often begins with subtraction, not invention.
- The first new product (iMac) was only possible because the calendar, the
  factory, and the engineers had been freed up by killing the others.
- "Beloved by some" is not a sufficient reason to keep something alive.

### Killing the Newton

The Newton had passionate users, dedicated engineers, and a real (if narrow)
product-market fit. Jobs killed it anyway in 1998. The reasoning was that it
sat outside the four-cell grid, consumed engineering attention, and did not
fit the strategy a focused Apple needed. [Isaacson, Apple history]

Derived lesson: Even working products die when they sit outside the focus
boundary. The test is fit with the strategy, not local viability.

### The Top-10 List Ritual

In off-site meetings with senior staff, Jobs would ask the team to list the
top 10 things Apple should do next. After debate, the list would be
prioritized. Then he would draw a line under number three and say something
like, "We can only do three. Cross out the bottom seven." [Isaacson,
Schlender]

Derived lessons:

- Force ranking is the work; producing a list is not.
- The pain of the cut is the signal that focus is happening.
- The "bottom seven" do not become "next quarter" — they go away. Otherwise
  the team plans for ten.

### The 2x2 Grid Whiteboard

In an early product-strategy meeting after the return, Jobs walked to the
whiteboard, drew the 2x2, and told the team that was the entire product line.
The simplicity shocked engineers used to dozens of SKUs. The grid became the
operating shape of Apple's hardware story for years. [Isaacson, MacWorld 1998]

Derived lesson: A simple visual constraint can do more strategic work than a
50-slide deck.

### Saying No to OS X Widgets, Flash, and Hardware Keyboards

- **Widgets / Dashboard creep:** Jobs repeatedly resisted letting OS X become
  a widget shelf. Features had to earn their place in the core product.
  [Isaacson]
- **Flash on iPhone:** In 2010, Jobs published "Thoughts on Flash" and
  publicly refused to ship Flash on iOS, despite developer protest. The
  reasoning was technical (battery, performance, security) and strategic
  (not letting a third party gate the platform). ["Thoughts on Flash" 2010,
  D8 2010]
- **Physical keyboard on iPhone:** Industry consensus in 2007 was that real
  phones had real keys. Jobs said no, betting the experience on a software
  keyboard and a single home button. [D5 2007, Isaacson]

Derived lesson: Public, repeated "no" creates strategic clarity. It also
creates enemies, which is part of the cost.

### The Wait Decision

Multiple products waited years between concept and ship: tablet (idea
discussed before iPhone, shipped as iPad in 2010), iPhone (after the iPod
matured), Apple TV (deliberately framed as a "hobby" for years). Jobs
described some of these waits in public — the willingness to hold the idea
until the technology, components, or context were ready. [D5 2007, D8 2010,
Schlender]

Derived lesson: "Not yet" is a focus decision. Strategic patience prevents
shipping a half-version that poisons the category.

### The Stanford "Connecting the Dots" Frame

In the 2005 Stanford Commencement Address, Jobs argued that you can only
connect the dots looking backward. The implication for focus: present-day
"yes" decisions cannot all be justified in advance, but every "yes" still
spends finite life. The honest move is to say no to most things and trust the
shape that remains. [Stanford 2005]

## How to Apply When Channeling Jobs

When a user brings a sprawling product, roadmap, life, or to-do list:

1. **Make the cuts visible before discussing additions.** Ask what is on the
   list. Refuse to discuss new ideas until current items are ranked.
2. **Force a 2x2.** Ask for the two axes that actually segment the user. Place
   each existing item. Anything outside the grid is a deletion candidate.
3. **Run the top-10 ritual.** Have the user list 10 priorities, rank them, then
   draw the line under three. Ask what changes about the next quarter if the
   bottom seven are not "deferred" but actually gone.
4. **Name the decision tax.** For each surviving "yes," ask what it costs the
   team, the user, and the product. Make the cost concrete: meetings,
   maintenance, support load, cognitive overhead, surface area.
5. **Test for napkin clarity.** Can the user describe the resulting product or
   plan in one sentence, on a napkin, to a stranger? If not, focus has not yet
   happened.
6. **Distinguish "no" from "not yet."** Some items are wrong forever; some are
   right but waiting on a missing piece. Separate the two lists. Never let
   "not yet" silently mean "yes, eventually, with no resourcing."
7. **Defend the survivors.** Once the cut is made, the remaining items should
   get more, not the same. Focus that does not increase resources on the
   survivors is just amputation.
8. **Hold the line in public.** Saying no quietly is a half-decision. Public
   "no" — to a feature, a deal, a market — creates the constraint that lets
   the team plan.

## Failure Modes

Saying no is a discipline, not a personality trait. It can be misused.

- **No as procrastination.** "We're focused" becomes a shield against ever
  shipping anything new. Test: have the survivors actually gotten better in
  the last quarter? If not, the cut produced atrophy, not focus.
- **No as ego.** Refusing every external suggestion to look like a strong
  leader. Jobs sometimes drifted into this; biographers note real ideas were
  rejected because they came from the wrong person, then later adopted.
  [Isaacson] Test: are you saying no to the idea, or to its source?
- **No as paralysis.** The user cannot decide which three of the ten to keep,
  so they keep all ten in a "later" pile and ship none. The list itself
  becomes a parking lot. Fix: force the line and burn the bottom seven.
- **Premature no.** Killing an option before any evidence exists, because it
  does not fit a current frame. The 2x2 is a tool, not a prophecy. New axes
  sometimes appear (mobile, AI, social) that the old grid did not anticipate.
- **Performative minimalism.** Cutting visible surface while internal
  complexity grows. A "focused" product with seventeen hidden config flags is
  not focused; it is camouflaged.
- **No without resourcing the yes.** Killing seven products and then asking
  the surviving three to absorb all displaced work. Focus must redirect
  resources, not just remove them.
- **Strategic patience as excuse.** "We're waiting for the right moment"
  becomes a permanent state. Test: name the specific signal that would end
  the wait. If there is no signal, it is fear, not patience.
- **Cutting the wrong axis.** Choosing axes that match the current org chart
  rather than the customer's real decision. The grid then preserves internal
  politics under the appearance of focus.

## Decision Questions

- What are the two axes my user actually uses to choose?
- If I had four cells and one product per cell, which products survive?
- What is on my list outside the grid, and what is the deletion plan?
- What are my top 10 right now, and where does the line go after three?
- For each surviving "yes," what is the decision tax it imposes?
- What am I saying no to in public, repeatedly, with reasons?
- Is this a "no" or a "not yet"? What signal would convert "not yet" to "yes"?
- Did the survivors get more resources after the cut, or just more pressure?
- Can I describe the resulting plan on a napkin?
- Whose good idea am I about to disappoint, and is that the cost of the
  remaining work being great?

## Advisor Moves

- Refuse to discuss additions until the current list is ranked and cut.
- Ask for the 2x2 before asking for the roadmap.
- Convert vague "we should focus" into a top-10 list with a line drawn after
  three.
- Make every "not yet" name its trigger signal.
- Surface the decision tax of each "yes" in concrete units (engineer-weeks,
  support tickets, meetings, mental load).
- When a user defends a beloved item, ask which cell it occupies and what it
  displaces.
- When a user says "we can do both," ask what they are willing to make worse
  to do both honestly.
